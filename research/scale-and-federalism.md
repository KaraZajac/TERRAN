# Scale: how governing scales, and what must be planetary

*Research note · planetary government, round 2 · first pass, 11 October 2026 · compiled by Claude, not yet reviewed by Kara*

Where checked, large polities list the centre's powers, assign the rest by a residual rule, and leave about 95% of binding acts to executives. Population size lowers participation, trust and citizens' sense of competence, so the planetary level should hold only goods with planetary spillovers. Universal votes on the questions a large polity decides would cost each adult tens to thousands of hours a year (estimate). The evidence favours a small planetary core decided by sampled assemblies under a citizen veto, with delivery kept close to people.

## What the Charter says now

- **Who decides.** Every terran votes "on the questions before the Commonwealth" (VII.3). A proposal "backed by enough terrans" goes to an assembly drawn by lot "to mirror humanity", which writes the ballot; every terran then votes (VIII.1–4).
- **Where.** Decisions are made "as close as possible to the people they affect"; planetary matters, "including the ceiling", go to all terrans (VII.4). No clause lists planetary powers or gives a residual rule.
- **Who administers.** A Council of Stewards, "elected by all terrans for one fixed term", administers with no veto and no army (VIII.7).
- **Checks.** A Planetary Court above community courts (IX.1), Guardians funded by a fixed share of planetary revenue (X.3, X.7), and an Ombudsman (VIII.9).
- **Stages.** Stage one brings the ceiling, the weapons handover and the Court (XII.2). At half of humanity come the floor, the wealth limit and planetary revenue (XII.3). At nine in ten, nations become "self-governing communities" (XII.4).
- **Absent.** No emergency procedure, no named tier between communities and the planet, and no cap on questions sent to all terrans.

Kara has since asked to drop universal votes on every question (steer of 11 October 2026). The estimate at the end shows why.

## Size and democracy

Dahl and Tufte argued that no single size suits democracy, because "citizen effectiveness" and "system capacity" pull apart [larsen/2002_municipal-size-and-democracy]. Their size effects ran on a log scale: 10,000 extra residents matter far more at 5,000 than at 120,000 [larsen/2002_municipal-size-and-democracy]. I could not open the book, so its own figures are not reported here [dahl-tufte/1973_size-and-democracy].

| Study | Setting | Finding |
|---|---|---|
| Larsen (2002) | 38 Danish municipalities; 50,765 respondents, 1994–97 | Participation higher in small municipalities; size did not affect interest, knowledge or trust in local politicians [larsen/2002_municipal-size-and-democracy] |
| Lassen and Serritzlew (2011) | Denmark's large municipal reform, surveys before and after | Size had "a causal and sizeable detrimental effect" on citizens' internal political efficacy [lassen-serritzlew/2011_jurisdiction-size-efficacy] |
| Blom-Hansen et al. (2016) | The same reform, difference-in-differences | Net effect on spending "null": savings in some areas offset by others [blom-hansen/2016_jurisdiction-size-expenditure] |
| Gerring and Veenendaal (2020) | Cross-country and within-country data | Participation falls with size at every level; trust in politicians falls; ethnic minorities are better represented in small units. Large units recruit more on merit and buy fewer votes. No link between population and democracy [anckar/2020_review-population-and-politics] |
| Alesina and Spolaore (1995) | Formal model | Scale economies trade against the costs of heterogeneity; democratisation leads to secessions [alesina-spolaore/1995_number-and-size-of-nations] |

I read Gerring and Veenendaal only through the publisher's page and a review, so their coefficients are not reported [gerring-veenendaal/2020_population-and-politics]. Dahl later wrote that "an international organization is not and probably cannot be a democracy" [dahl/1999_can-international-organizations-be-democratic].

Assemblies scale too. Taagepera's 1972 "cube-root law" ties assembly size to the cube root of population [epfl/2021_cube-root-law]. For 8,300.7 million people in mid-2026, that gives about 2,025 seats of 4.1 million people each (estimate) [un-desa/2024_wpp-population-by-age]. At that size, 108 of 237 countries and areas get under one expected seat (own calculation, same data). Margaritondo calls the law's derivation "conceptually flawed" and power laws unreliable for political analysis [margaritondo/2021_cube-root-law-flawed]. Local seat totals track the number of jurisdictions (elasticity 2/3) more than population (1/3) [le-maux-paty/2024_cube-root-local-assemblies].

**Design implication:** Each step up in scale costs efficacy, participation and trust, and mergers rarely save money. A planetary level should be narrow, and its legitimacy must come from procedure, not closeness.

**Design implication:** Large legislatures decide about one binding act in twenty; executives and committees make the rest. A planetary charter must say who makes the other 95%, and under what control.

## Multi-level government at the largest scales

**India.** Parliament holds residuary powers (Art. 248) and may legislate on state matters after a two-thirds Rajya Sabha resolution (Art. 249) [india/1950_constitution]. The 73rd and 74th amendments (1992) list 29 rural and 18 urban local matters (Arts. 243G, 243W) [india/1950_constitution]. But a state only "may, by law, endow" local bodies with those powers [india/1950_constitution]. A third of seats and chair posts are reserved for women (Art. 243D) [india/1950_constitution]. On 11 October 2026 the national directory listed 262,836 rural local bodies, including 255,392 village panchayats, plus 5,050 urban ones [lgd/2026_local-government-directory]. About 1.4 million women held elected seats in panchayats and rural local bodies in January 2025, 46% of the total [president-of-india/2025_pri-women-representatives]. That implies about 3 million elected members (estimate: 1.4 ÷ 0.46). When West Bengal randomly reserved a third of village council chairs for women, those councils invested more in water, fuel and roads [chattopadhyay-duflo/2004_women-as-policy-makers].

**EU.** National parliaments can force a review ("yellow card") with a third of votes; it was triggered three times, and the "orange card" never [european-parliament/2026_subsidiarity-factsheet]. A 2021 Parliament paper found the Court "has never struck down a measure" for breaching subsidiarity [european-parliament/2026_subsidiarity-factsheet]. Qualified majority is the Council's default: 55% of members holding 65% of population [ep/2026_council-factsheet]. Taxation, social security, the budget framework and treaty change still need unanimity [ep/2026_council-factsheet]. In 2024, 368 committees of member-state officials, chaired by the Commission, cleared 1,938 implementing acts [european-commission/2025_comitology-report-2024]. Committees gave "no opinion" 21 times; the Appeal Committee did so on all 11 drafts it saw, and the Commission adopted all 11 [european-commission/2025_comitology-report-2024]. About 33,000 staff serve the Commission [european-commission/2026_commission-staff], for over 450 million people [wikipedia/2024_european-parliament-election]: one per 13,600 people (estimate).

Nigeria also requires state ratification of treaty laws on non-federal matters (s. 12) [nigeria/1999_constitution]. Its president needs a quarter of votes in two-thirds of states (s. 134) [nigeria/1999_constitution]. Worldwide there were 663,639 subnational governments in 135 countries, including 2,513 state or regional ones (2020 data) [sng-wofi/2022_synthesis-highlights]. They made 21.5% of public spending: 41.9% in federal countries, 17.1% in unitary ones [sng-wofi/2022_synthesis-highlights].

**Design implication:** Federations list the centre's powers and leave the rest below, but bottom tiers depend on middle tiers for money. Where a constitution says "may", devolution stalls. A charter must fix the bottom tier's powers and revenue itself.

## Decentralisation: theory and evidence

- **Oates' theorem.** Without cost savings from centralisation or spillovers between jurisdictions, responsibilities should be decentralised [koethenbuerger/2007_decentralization-theorem]. Centralisation wins when spillovers are high enough [koethenbuerger/2007_decentralization-theorem]. I read only the abstract of Oates' own survey [oates/1999_essay-fiscal-federalism].
- **Who redistributes.** The Musgrave–Oates model gives redistribution and stabilisation to the centre, because mobile capital and residents defeat local redistribution [majocchi/2008_fiscal-federalism-european-experience]. The EU departs from it: both stay mostly national [majocchi/2008_fiscal-federalism-european-experience].
- **Outcomes.** Treisman found "almost no solidly established, general empirical findings" [channa-faguet/2016_decentralization-health-education]. Weighting studies by quality, Channa and Faguet find decentralisation raises service efficiency, from test scores to infant mortality [channa-faguet/2016_decentralization-health-education].
- **Bailouts.** Lower tiers that rely on central transfers but can borrow invite bailouts; tax autonomy makes no-bailout promises credible [rodden/2006_hamiltons-paradox].
- **A constitutional version.** Swiss cantons hold every power not given to the federation (Art. 3) [switzerland/1999_federal-constitution]. Art. 43a adds: "The collective body that benefits from a public service bears the costs thereof" [switzerland/1999_federal-constitution].

**Design implication:** "Planetary" can be tested: planetary spillovers, real economies of scale, or mobility that defeats local redistribution. Everything else goes down, and whoever pays decides.

## Speed with safety at scale

- **EU.** Ordinary laws took 20 months on average in 2019–2024, 24 without urgent cases [european-parliament/2024_olp-activity-report]. The urgent procedure covered 61 of 415 files (about 15%) and averaged 1.7 months [european-parliament/2024_olp-activity-report].
- **Switzerland.** Urgent federal acts take effect at once but must be time-limited [switzerland/1999_federal-constitution]. If a referendum is requested and voters do not approve within a year, the act lapses and cannot be renewed (Art. 165) [switzerland/1999_federal-constitution]. Voters ratified the COVID-19 Act three times: 60.2% (June 2021), 62.0% (November 2021) and 61.9% (June 2023) [swissvotes/2026_dataset].
- **Treaty route.** The WHO Pandemic Agreement was adopted on 20 May 2025 [who/2025_pandemic-agreement-qa]. In May 2026 the World Health Assembly extended talks on its pathogen-sharing annex to its next session or an earlier special session [paho/2026_igwg-pabs-annex]. The agreement is still not open for signature.

**Design implication:** Fast tracks work when narrow and self-expiring: act now, ratify within a year, lapse if refused. Treaty-style planetary rule-making is too slow for emergencies.

## What must be planetary: global public goods

Kaul, Grunberg and Stern define global public goods as outcomes that "tend towards universality", benefiting all countries, groups and generations [kaul-grunberg-stern/1999_global-public-goods]. How a good aggregates decides how hard it is to supply [kaul-grunberg-stern/1999_global-public-goods]:

| Type | Example in the source | Problem |
|---|---|---|
| Summation | Emission cuts: a ton saved in Bangladesh equals one in France | Free riding |
| Weakest link | Disease surveillance; financial supervision | The least capable member sets the level |
| Best shot | Advanced medicine; fast emergency response | Risk of a "hegemonic model" of provision |

Barrett's book adds mutual restraint, "agreeing what states ought not to do", and coordination on standards [barrett/2007_why-cooperate]. The editors warn that over-centralised provision can "stifle national ownership" [kaul-grunberg-stern/1999_global-public-goods]. They prefer a "jurisdictional loop" through national and regional levels to stronger supranational government [kaul-grunberg-stern/1999_global-public-goods]. The 2006 Task Force named six priorities: infectious disease, climate, financial stability, trade, peace and security, and knowledge [itf-gpg/2006_meeting-global-challenges]. It wrote: "For good reasons, there is no world government with the authority to tax, to conscript, to regulate or to quarantine" [itf-gpg/2006_meeting-global-challenges].

The record, as of October 2026:

- **A treaty can reach every state.** The Montreal Protocol has 198 parties; its Kigali Amendment has 175 [unep-ozone/2026_ratification-status].
- **Summation fails on pledges.** With new pledges from 113 parties, global 2035 emissions are projected about 12% below 2019 [unfccc/2025_ndc-synthesis-update].
- **Dues-funded centres are fragile.** The UN cut its 2026 budget by 15%, to $3.45 billion, abolishing 2,900 posts [un-news/2025_regular-budget-2026]. Unpaid dues hit a record $1.57 billion at the end of 2025 [cp24/2026_guterres-un-financial-collapse].

**Design implication:** Planetary means a good that fails without near-universal participation or restraint. Summation and weakest-link goods need binding shares, verification and own revenue; best-shot goods need funding and limits on unilateral action.

## Feasibility benchmarks for a planetary vote

| | India, 2024 | European Parliament, 2024 |
|---|---|---|
| Electorate | 977,965,560 registered [wikipedia/2024_indian-general-election] | About 357 million eligible [wikipedia/2024_european-parliament-election] |
| Turnout | 646.4 million votes, 66.1% [wikipedia/2024_indian-general-election] | 50.74%, against 61.99% in 1979 [european-parliament/2024_election-turnout] |
| Duration | Seven phases, 19 April–1 June, counted 4 June [wikipedia/2024_indian-general-election] | 6–9 June [wikipedia/2024_european-parliament-election] |
| Logistics | Over a million polling stations, 5.5 million voting machines, 15 million staff [wikipedia/2024_indian-general-election]; 39 repolls [newsonair/2024_cec-world-record] | 720 seats across 27 states [wikipedia/2024_european-parliament-election] |
| Cost | ₹1.35 lakh crore by all actors, the Election Commission's share 10–15% (projection) [national-herald/2024_costliest-election] | Not found |

At ₹83.67 per dollar (2024 average) [worldbank/2026_wdi-exchange-rate-india], that is $16.1 billion in all, and $1.65–2.47 of official cost per registered voter (estimate). India has no postal or online voting for its diaspora [wikipedia/2024_indian-general-election].

A planetary vote would involve about 5.9 billion adults: everyone aged 20 and over, plus two-fifths of those aged 15–19 (estimate) [un-desa/2024_wpp-population-by-age]. That is 6.0 times India's register. At India's ratios it needs about 6.0 million polling stations, 90 million staff and 33 million machines. Official costs would be $9.7–14.6 billion, or about $97 billion with campaign spending (estimates). Online-only voting would miss the 2.2 billion people still offline [itu/2025_facts-and-figures]. About 800 million people lack any official ID [worldbank-id4d/2025_global-dataset].

**Design implication:** A planetary popular vote is a rare, costly event, about six Indian general elections at once. It suits constitutional choices and vetoes, not routine law.

## Design patterns that work

- **A short planetary list, a residual rule, and payer decides.** Switzerland and Nigeria list the centre's powers and leave the rest below [switzerland/1999_federal-constitution; nigeria/1999_constitution]. *Condition:* an umpire that actually rules. *Failure:* the EU's court has never voided a measure on subsidiarity grounds [european-parliament/2026_subsidiarity-factsheet].
- **Sortition in place of universal votes.** Panels of 1,000 deliberating 100 hours each on 83 questions a year take 8.3 million person-hours (estimate). *Condition:* statistical size, parallel panels per question, regional draws, a published method ([deliberation](deliberation.md)). *Failure:* legitimacy; Swiss voters rejected judges by lot 68% to 32% ([accountability](accountability.md)), and 108 of 237 areas fall below one seat at 2,025 seats.
- **Delegation with a citizen veto.** In Switzerland, 50,000 signatures in 100 days force a vote on a federal act; most acts take effect without one [switzerland-bk/2026_referenden]. The dataset records 219 such votes since 1875; the act survived 127 of the 218 decided, 58% (own calculation) [swissvotes/2026_dataset]. *Failure:* at planetary scale, organised money could decide which acts get challenged (inference).
- **Delegated rules under member committees, with drafts published.** EU committees cleared 1,938 acts in 2024, and 108 drafts went out for four weeks' comment [european-commission/2025_comitology-report-2024]. *Failure:* contested acts end in "no opinion", and the executive decides alone [european-commission/2025_comitology-report-2024].
- **Continuous feedback, weighted by verified identity or sampling.** US agencies must publish proposed rules and take written comment [us/1946_apa-section-553]; 1,498–2,149 proposals a year went out in 2019–2025 [federal-register/2026_documents-api]. *Failure:* nearly 18 million of more than 22 million comments in one 2017 proceeding were fake [ny-ag/2021_fake-comments-report]. Count verified or sampled voices, or map opinion clusters as vTaiwan did ([deliberation](deliberation.md)), not raw volume.
- **Act now, ratify within a year, lapse if refused.** Swiss Art. 165 and the EU urgent procedure deliver law in weeks [switzerland/1999_federal-constitution; european-parliament/2024_olp-activity-report]. *Failure:* serial renewal, which Switzerland bans after a refusal.
- **Planetary money, local delivery, local trials.** Redistribution centralises; delivery decentralises; random rollouts teach [majocchi/2008_fiscal-federalism-european-experience; channa-faguet/2016_decentralization-health-education; chattopadhyay-duflo/2004_women-as-policy-makers]. *Failure:* middle tiers capture funds, as in Nigeria [arise/2026_lg-autonomy-26-months]. Experimentalist governance belongs to the seventh strand.
- **Spread rules for single planetary choices.** Nigeria's quarter-in-two-thirds rule and the EU's 55%/65% double majority stop one region deciding [nigeria/1999_constitution; ep/2026_council-factsheet]. *Failure:* blocking minorities.
- **Machine-readable registers.** Both registers counted here answer by year and type through public interfaces [eur-lex/2026_legal-acts-statistics; federal-register/2026_documents-api]. That makes a published "decision budget" cheap; rules as code are for the seventh strand.

## Tensions and open problems

- **The flow chart still sends everything to all.** Part VIII's diagram ends in "Every terran votes". Dropping VII.3 means deciding which questions, if any, still go to everyone.
- **Sortition's legitimacy is unproven at binding scale.** Voters have rejected it for judges, and Dahl doubts any international body can be democratic.
- **Scale erodes voice.** No source found shows digital channels reversing the size effects on efficacy and trust.
- **Redistribution.** Theory puts it at the top because of mobility; the EU keeps it national. Free movement (VIII.10) and exit (XII.5) strengthen the mobility case.
- **Heterogeneity drives secession**, yet exit ends at stage three (XII.5).
- **No umpire for "planetary".** Courts rarely enforce subsidiarity, and the Planetary Court already carries a heavy load (pressure point 5).
- **No middle tier.** Middle tiers capture lower ones in India and Nigeria, but without one a planetary centre faces nearly 650,000 municipal governments [sng-wofi/2022_synthesis-highlights].
- **The ceiling is a summation good without shares.**
- **Not verified:** Dahl–Tufte and Gerring–Veenendaal coefficients; Taagepera's original paper; Oates', Barrett's and Treisman's full texts. Also unchecked: the Centre for Media Studies cost report, the share of Swiss acts challenged by referendum, and India's devolution index.

## Options for the next draft

- **VII.3.** Replace the universal vote with: "Terran Assemblies drawn by lot make planetary law. Any law goes to all terrans if enough terrans, spread across regions, ask within a set period."
- **VII.4.** List planetary powers (the core below), add "all other powers belong to communities", and add Swiss-style fiscal equivalence: whoever benefits pays, whoever pays decides.
- **An umpire.** Let a set share of communities object that a measure is not planetary. An assembly or the Court then rules within a fixed time, and the objection binds, unlike the EU's yellow card.
- **VIII.3.** Replace "mirror humanity" with a statistical standard: at least 1,000 members, named strata, a published method. Run assemblies in parallel, perhaps drawn through about 2,000 regions of 4 million.
- **VIII.7.** Have Stewards make implementing rules through committees of community delegates, publish drafts for verified comment, and face assembly objection. Consider having a Terran Assembly drawn by lot choose the Stewards, rather than a planetary election.
- **An emergency clause.** Stewards may adopt urgent measures for at most a year. An assembly must ratify them within a set period, and a refused measure cannot be renewed.
- **XII.3.** Make the floor a planetary guarantee and transfer, delivered by communities, funded by capped own revenue on multi-year cycles.
- **New stage-one or stage-two powers.** Pandemic and other weakest-link minimum capacities; a coordination-standards function.
- **A decision budget.** Publish every planetary act in a machine-readable register, and cap questions sent to all terrans, for example at ten a year.

## The decision-load estimate

**Inputs.** About 5.9 billion adults (above). The March 2026 Swiss voting booklet ran to 72 pages and about 15,000 words for four proposals and six ballot questions [switzerland-bk/2026_erlaeuterungen-maerz-2026]. At 238 words a minute [brysbaert/2019_reading-rate], that is 63 minutes, or 10–16 minutes a question. So 0.25 hours a question covers reading the summary; 1 hour allows some discussion.

**Universal voting (estimate).** Hours = questions a year × 0.25–1 hour; total = hours × 5.9 billion adults.

| If the planet decided like | Questions a year | Hours per adult a year | Person-hours a year |
|---|---|---|---|
| Swiss federal votes | 8.6 | 2–9 | 13–51 billion |
| India's Parliament | 36 | 9–36 | 53–212 billion |
| EU ordinary procedure | 83 | 21–83 | 122–490 billion |
| US Congress | 159 | 40–159 | 235–938 billion |
| All EU acts | 2,160–2,589 | 540–2,589 | 3.2–15.3 trillion |
| All US final rules | 3,168 | 792–3,168 | 4.7–18.7 trillion |

A full-time working year is about 1,920 hours (48 weeks of 40). Swiss turnout averaged 48.8% in 2016–2025, at about nine questions a year [swissvotes/2026_dataset]. Longer ballots raise abstention and status-quo votes. Without that fatigue, abstention would fall 8% and 6% of propositions in one dataset would have passed, not failed [augenblick-nicholson/2016_choice-fatigue].

**Sortition (estimate).** 83 questions × 1,000 members × 100 hours = 8.3 million person-hours a year, about 5 seconds per adult. Each adult has a 1 in 71,000 chance of serving in a year. The 100 hours exceed the 68 that the 2021 Global Assembly met ([deliberation](deliberation.md)).

On these numbers, VII.3 holds only for a Swiss-sized agenda of about ten questions a year. Anything near a legislature's load needs delegation. On the same 83 questions, sampling uses 15,000 to 59,000 times less citizen time than universal voting (estimate).
