# Legislatures by lot: sortition at planetary scale

*Research note · planetary government, round 2 · first pass, 11 October 2026 · compiled by Claude, not yet reviewed by Kara*

No legislature chosen by lot has ever held full lawmaking power; today's standing allotted bodies advise, and take one to three topics a year. Their weak points grow with scale: only 2–12% of invitees say yes, staff and experts shape texts, and Madrid's body died with its majority. Online, AI-moderated deliberation has reached 6,300 people in 19 languages, though its small groups stayed within one language and bound no one. The evidence favours many narrow, entrenched allotted bodies with split roles, staggered rotation and public lotteries over one assembly that "mirrors humanity".

## What the Charter says now

- **VII.3 and VIII.4.** Every terran votes on every question, on the exact text an assembly wrote. Kara's round-2 steer takes this off the table.
- **VII.4.** Decisions sit as close as possible to those affected; planetary matters go to all.
- **VIII.1 and VIII.3.** A proposal "backed by enough terrans" goes to an assembly "drawn by lot to mirror humanity". It studies the proposal with experts and Guardians, may improve it, and writes the ballot.
- **VIII.8.** "Terrans and their assemblies make law."
- **IX.2, X.3, XI.5.** Lot also picks the judges' selectors, half the Guardians, and a review assembly every 25 years.

So the draft uses lot to draft, not to decide, and convenes one assembly per proposal. Round 1 ([deliberation](deliberation.md)) covered Ireland, the OECD's count, the Global Assembly's basics, France's climate convention and margins of error.

## Five blueprints for a legislature by lot

| Design | Agenda | Drafting | Final say | Size, term, pay |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Guerrero, 2021 [guerrero/2021_single-issue-legislatures] | Each single-issue body sets its own, with expert, stakeholder and polling input | The same body, with drafting aides and public comment | The same body | 30 bodies of 300 by voluntary lottery; three-year terms, 100 replaced yearly; "perhaps around $500,000 each year" |
| Guerrero, *Lottocracy*, 2024 [greene/2026_review-guerrero-lottocracy] | As above; "structural assemblies" coordinate | As above | 20 single-issue bodies of 450; executive assemblies implement | Lifetime odds of serving about 12% |
| Gastil and Wright, 2018–19 [gastil/2018_legislature-by-lot; buge/2019_assemblee-parlementaire-tiree-au-sort] | Both chambers | Committees with staff and expert hearings | An elected and an allotted chamber, equal in power | Two or five years; same pay as elected members; internal removal for misconduct |
| Landemore, 2020 [landemore/2020_open-democracy] | An "open mini-public", plus "participatory rights" for all | The mini-public, linked to crowdsourcing platforms | The mini-public, by majority | 150 to 1,000 members, for "a few days to a few years" |
| Abizadeh, 2021 [abizadeh/2021_sortition-second-chamber] | Not verified | Not verified | An elected chamber, reviewed and checked by an allotted one | Would replace Canada's Senate |
| Bouricius, 2013 [bouricius/2013_multi-body-sortition] | Allotted Agenda Council, plus petitions | Self-selected panels of about 12; an allotted Review Panel of about 150 amends | Policy Juries of 400 or more per bill, by secret ballot without debate | Review Panels full-time for about three years; separate Rules and Oversight Councils |

The designs split on whether one body does everything. Bouricius divides agenda, drafting, review, decision, rules and staff oversight, trading jury representativeness against council commitment [bouricius/2013_multi-body-sortition]. Guerrero concedes four open problems: coherence, budgeting, lost cross-issue bargaining and drawing issue boundaries. He proposes "meta-assemblies" for budget and oversight [guerrero/2021_single-issue-legislatures]. His 2014 article could not be opened [guerrero/2014_against-elections].

Critics are plain. Mansbridge: "I would oppose any legislative system based entirely on lot. Too much of value to democracy would be lost." No fully empowered allotted assembly has existed anywhere, even in Athens, and most contributors favour gradual steps [buge/2019_assemblee-parlementaire-tiree-au-sort]. A 2026 review warns: "Under lottocracy, there is no obvious outlet for mass movements of reform." [greene/2026_review-guerrero-lottocracy]. Lafont and Urbinati argue that a "lottocratic mentality" undermines electoral representation [lafont/2024_lottocratic-mentality].

**Design implication:** Split agenda, drafting, decision and oversight across bodies with stated sizes. No blueprint has tested the budget and coherence layer.

## Standing bodies by lot in practice

| Body | Size and selection | Power | Output | Status, October 2026 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ostbelgien Bürgerdialog (2019–) | Council of 24 former members; assemblies of 25–50, about 30 in practice | Advisory; parliament must answer | Eight assemblies, 2020–2026 | Running; the eighth meets in autumn 2026 [buergerdialog/2026_stand-der-dinge] |
| Paris Assemblée citoyenne (2021–) | 100 a year: 80 from electoral rolls, 15 non-EU residents, 5 aged 16–17 | Proposes motions and draft decisions to the Council of Paris | Three cohorts, seven topics, all adopted | Unclear after the March 2026 election |
| Brussels deliberative committees (rules 2019) | 45 citizens and 15 MPs | Joint recommendations; citizens' votes consultative | Six committees by February 2024 | Not verified after February 2024 |
| EU Conference on the Future of Europe (2021–22) | Four panels of 200 | Fed a plenary in which institutions could object | 49 proposals, 326 measures | Ended |
| European Citizens' Panels (after the Conference) | About 150, at least two per state, a third aged 16–29 | Advise the Commission | Eight concluded panels listed | Running [european-commission/2026_european-citizens-panels] |
| Madrid Observatorio de la Ciudad (2019) | 49 members and 49 substitutes; one-year mandate | Monitor the city and recommend | Four sessions in 2019 | Abolished February 2020 |

**Ostbelgien.** Positive replies fell from 115 of 1,000 letters (11.5%) at the first lottery to 75 of 2,000 (3.75%) at the seventh. (Its table prints 3.84%; the counts give 3.75%.) Young people and non-graduates replied least. In the first three assemblies, 16 of 25, 14 of 25 and 21 of 30 members attended the last meeting. For the first assembly, an expert group counted 5 of 14 recommendations implemented and 9 partly. The 2025 review says impact still has no objective measure; since 2023 the government must report yearly. A 2022 evaluation judged the decree's maximum of three assemblies a year too heavy and recommended one [pdg/2025_buergerdialog-evaluation-2019-2024]. A 2024 amendment capped them at five per parliamentary term [ostbelgien/2019_buergerdialog-decree]. The 2019 budget was €90,000 [niessen/2022_institutionalising-citizen-deliberation]. Feared political steering of topics did not materialise [pdg/2025_buergerdialog-evaluation-2019-2024].

**Paris.** Three cohorts took seven topics, and the Council of Paris adopted every motion and draft decision [paris/2025_livret-suivi-promotion-1; paris/2025_livret-suivi-promotion-2]. For the third cohort, a bailiff witnessed the July 2024 lottery. Its drafting group chose among four topics proposed by the Mayor and deputies. Members, administration and executive then "co-validated" the measures before the July 2025 vote [paris/2025_assemblee-citoyenne-promotion-3]. Follow-up booklets mark each recommendation done, in progress or not done. The new deputy mayor's remit of March 2026 names citizens' conventions and referendums, not the Assembly [paris/2026_adjoints-du-maire]. I found no source on a fourth cohort.

**Brussels.** Each committee starts from 10,000 letters and a second draw stratified by gender, age, district, language and education. Topics come from a 1,000-signature petition or from political groups. MPs report back in public after six to nine months [oecd-opsi/2022_brussels-deliberative-committees]. Six committees were held by February 2024 [parlement-bruxelles/2024_commissions-deliberatives-colloque], fewer than the "three times a year" planned. The 2023 Shortcut, co-written by parliament staff, says recommendations are "often implemented" but gives no count [bertelsmann/2023_shortcut-9-deliberative-committees]. I found no independent tally.

**The EU.** Kantar recruited the Conference's 800 citizens mostly by random-digit dialling. Polish and Czech recruitment lagged "mainly due to low levels of trust" [technopolis/2024_cofoe-citizens-panels-study]. The Parliament's own May 2024 count found 243 of 326 measures (75%) fully or partly addressed. The 31 measures needing Treaty change remained pending. Uptake was strongest where ideas "converged with the more general political agenda" [european-parliament/2024_cofe-follow-up-in-numbers].

**Madrid.** The city invited 30,000 of its 1.29 million households to a second, stratified draw [madrid/2019_observatorio-invitacion-hogares]. A new governing coalition of PP and Ciudadanos, backed by Vox, moved to repeal the body in December 2019. Its stated aim was a participation model centred on digital tools [madrid/2019_derogacion-observatorio-proyecto]. A committee approved repeal 8 votes to 6 on party lines [madrid/2020_diario-sesiones-comision-vicealcaldia]. The council followed on 25 February 2020, after the vice-mayor said 49 people chosen at random could not statistically represent Madrid [madrid/2020_pleno-transformacion-observatorio]. Its four 2019 sessions had cost €144,259 [madrid/2019_memoria-impacto-derogacion].

**Design implication:** Every body here advises, and uptake is partial and follows existing agendas. A new majority can repeal bodies set up by ordinary rules. So the Charter must entrench them and say what happens to their output.

## Selection at planetary scale

| Process | Invitations | Replies or picks | Seats |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ostbelgien, seventh assembly | 2,000 letters | 75 yes (3.75%) | About 30 |
| Brussels committee | 10,000 letters | Not given in sources read | 45 |
| Madrid | 30,000 households | Not given in sources read | 49 |
| France's climate convention [giraudet/2022_co-construction-ccc] | 300,000 random phone numbers | 11,400 reached; 190 picked | 150 |
| Global Assembly [global-assembly/2022_report-2021] | 100 random points, door-to-door | 20 of 100 first picks replaced | 100 |

Flanigan and colleagues report that "average response rates are typically between 2 and 5%". Volunteers over-represent groups such as college graduates, so quotas force unequal chances. Across ten real panels, the LEXIMIN algorithm raised the lowest chance to 26–65% (median 49%) of the equal-chance ideal. The older algorithm left some volunteers near zero. Under standard complexity assumptions, quota-compliant selection cannot run in polynomial time; one pool of 1,727 for 110 seats took 67 minutes. A live public lottery let each volunteer check their own chance [flanigan/2021_fair-algorithms-citizens-assemblies]. Such algorithms equalise chances among volunteers only. A related method reweights by willingness estimated from population surveys [flanigan/2020_neutralizing-self-selection].

**Size.** Precision depends on seats, not population. At 95% confidence, the margin on a 50/50 question is ±9.8 points at 100 seats and ±4.6 at 450. It is ±3.1 at 1,000 and ±1.2 at 6,750 (own calculation, 1.96 × √(0.25/n)). Small groups are harder. Of 8.14 billion people in 2024 [worldbank/2026_wdi-population], 0.1% is about 8.1 million. A random 450-seat body has a 64% chance of seating none of them; 1,000 seats, 37%; 6,750 seats, 0.1% (own calculation, 0.999ⁿ).

**Nesting.** If each stage draws evenly from the stage below, everyone keeps an equal chance; skew enters wherever people must volunteer again. Ostbelgien's council, drawn from volunteering former members, has under-represented young people and southern residents [pdg/2025_buergerdialog-evaluation-2019-2024]. The Global Assembly's local hosts sometimes broke protocol, with "targeted recruitment, self-selection, and one case of unreported replacement" [global-assembly/2022_report-2021].

**Design implication:** Name the frame, traits, algorithm, public lottery, replacement rule and audit. Draw planetary seats from the whole population, and size deciding bodies at 450–1,000 or more.

## Online deliberation, translation and rotation

**Online deliberation.** In December 2022, Stanford's Deliberative Democracy Lab ran a weekend deliberative poll for Meta. It drew over 6,300 people from 32 countries, in 19 languages [stanford-ddl/2023_metaverse-community-forum]. An AI-assisted platform moderated 2,069 small groups: it ran the speaking queue, nudged quiet members and intervened on incivility. The organisers estimate that people alone would have needed over 500 moderators. 78% said their group "participated relatively equally", and 73% said the platform tried to air opposing arguments. The limits are as plain. Participants were social media users, mostly from online panels, and Meta, whose policies were at stake, co-ran it. Plenaries of over 1,000 left little room for questions.

In a 2024 *Science* study, 5,734 participants preferred an AI mediator's group statements to human mediators'. The result held in a virtual citizens' assembly of UK residents [tessler/2024_ai-common-ground] (abstract only). Offline gaps remain. Internet failures were "a significant disruptive force" in the Global Assembly, and about 34% of members found its online whiteboard hard to use [global-assembly/2022_report-2021]. The Conference's multilingual digital platform barely entered the panels' deliberation [technopolis/2024_cofoe-citizens-panels-study].

**Translation at scale.** Human interpretation works, at a cost in pace. The Conference used all 24 EU languages; discussion slowed, and subgroups were limited to four or five languages. Its evaluators suggested single-language sessions first [technopolis/2024_cofoe-citizens-panels-study]. The Global Assembly's 100 members spoke 39 languages, and 64% needed translation. Most sat with an in-person translator, from the host organisation or a third party. Some translators intervened when it was unclear whether they were translating "or were attempting to represent themselves" [global-assembly/2022_report-2021]. Stanford sidestepped the problem: small groups shared a language and region, and only plenaries were interpreted. Its Sub-Saharan sample deliberated in English, and small islands with their own languages were excluded [stanford-ddl/2023_metaverse-community-forum]. I found no evaluation of machine translation inside live deliberation.

**Continuous rotation.** Ostbelgien's decree set 18-month council terms, a third replaced every six months. In practice the rhythm slipped, and rotation now follows each assembly. At times too few former members volunteered, and the council lacked young and southern members. Since 2023 applicants have exceeded seats, allowing a stratified draw and a reserve list. Belgium's Council of State holds that giving an allotted council regulatory powers breaches rule-of-law principles, for lack of parliamentary control. The 2024 reform moved those powers to parliament bodies [pdg/2025_buergerdialog-evaluation-2019-2024]. Guerrero's staggered thirds remain a proposal [guerrero/2021_single-issue-legislatures]. Athens' yearly pool of 6,000, with sub-panels drawn per case, is history [bouricius/2013_multi-body-sortition].

**Design implication:** Online, machine-moderated small groups can widen deliberation cheaply, given hosted access for the unconnected and audited platforms. Group speakers by language and interpret where groups meet. Rotate in small steps, filling a reserve list at every draw.

## Accountability and capture

**Without elections.** Practice offers rotation, witnessed lotteries, follow-up duties and recorded dissent: Ostbelgien's assemblies decide by consensus or four-fifths, with dissent attached [ostbelgien/2019_buergerdialog-decree]. Proposals add pay conditional on no prohibited contacts [guerrero/2021_single-issue-legislatures] and internal removal [buge/2019_assemblee-parlementaire-tiree-au-sort]. Bouricius adds an allotted Oversight Council that can hire and fire staff [bouricius/2013_multi-body-sortition].

**Capture.** France's climate convention is the best-documented case [giraudet/2022_co-construction-ccc]:

- A governance committee framed five groups around energy demand, leaving nuclear power "largely unaddressed". It invited experts "mostly at its discretion".
- Observers saw experts "unduly pushing for certain measures or discarding others". A six-member legal group rewrote proposals between sessions.
- Members helped draft only about a fifth of the measures they voted on. Block votes "contributed to generating high approval rates".
- Drafting went confidential from session 6, and about 3,400 public contributions went unused.
- Yet members overrode organisers twice: they dropped the carbon tax, and 60–80% refused referendums on technical measures.

In Ireland, an Independent TD objected to topics "generally beloved of the NGO and academic class" [offaly-express/2022_citizens-assemblies-costs].

**Public acceptance.**

- Across 15 West European countries in 2020 (14,043 respondents), support for allotted bodies deciding instead of politicians stood at 4.32 out of 10. About 18% answered 0. Support was highest among less-educated, anti-elite respondents, and partly outcome-driven [pilet/2023_public-support-sortition-15-countries].
- In a German experiment (2,039 people, December 2020), most preferred advisory forums working with existing institutions, large in size and needing clear majorities. A favourable outcome added 6.5 points [goldberg/2023_catching-deliberative-wave].
- In Northern Ireland, non-participants judged an assembly more legitimate when its members seemed "like me" [pow/2020_like-me-perceptions].
- French majorities backed a permanent 150-member assembly with a veto (68%, then 63%). Only 32%, then 28%, trusted random citizens on complex issues [giraudet/2022_co-construction-ccc].
- In 2017, 77% of Belgian MPs surveyed opposed an allotted second chamber, against 40% of citizens [buge/2019_assemblee-parlementaire-tiree-au-sort]. Swiss voters rejected federal judges by lot, 2,335,148 to 1,094,989 (68.1%), in November 2021 [switzerland/2022_federal-council-decree-vote-results].
- Invitees decline from focus on private life, feeling politically incompetent, avoiding public meetings, schedules, alienation and doubt about impact [jacquet/2017_explaining-non-participation]. Ostbelgien's 2026 decliners added fear of talking politics, and not knowing what the dialogue is [baglietto/2026_bv7-beobachtungsbericht].

**Design implication:** With no re-election, accountability must come from process: witnessed lotteries, open proceedings, rotation, follow-up duties and allotted oversight of staff. Support is conditional and highest for advisory bodies, so binding power needs review and a public veto.

## Costs and throughput

| Process | Cost | Scale | Cost per member-day (estimate) |
|---|---|---|---|
| France's climate convention, 2019–21 | €6.7 million [giraudet/2022_co-construction-ccc] | 150 members, eight three-day sessions | About €1,861: €6.7m ÷ (150 × 24) |
| Global Assembly, 2021 | US$972,247 [global-assembly/2022_report-2021] | 100 members, 68 hours online | US$143 per member-hour; about US$858 per six-hour day |
| Madrid, 2019 | €144,259 [madrid/2019_memoria-impacto-derogacion] | 49 members, four sessions | About €736 per member-session |
| Ostbelgien, 2019 | €90,000 budget [niessen/2022_institutionalising-citizen-deliberation] | One or two assemblies plus council | Not computed |
| Ireland, 2014–2021 | €4.1 million, reported from parliamentary answers [offaly-express/2022_citizens-assemblies-costs] | Several assemblies | Not computed |

Member pay is a small share. France paid €84 a day; 150 × €1,462 is about 3% of cost (estimate) [giraudet/2022_co-construction-ccc]. Global Assembly stipends were a flat US$600, 6% of spending; hosts took 29% and coordination 34% [global-assembly/2022_report-2021]. Ostbelgien pays €37.50 per meeting, indexed and doubled beyond four hours [ostbelgien/2019_buergerdialog-decree].

**Throughput.** Each allotted body takes one or two major topics a year. Ostbelgien took eight in about six years, Paris seven in four, Brussels six in four. A topic can still yield many items: 45 recommendations on housing in Ostbelgien [pdg/2023_wohnen-abschlussbericht], 149 measures in France. The UK Parliament passed 57 Acts in 2023, 25 in 2024 (an election year) and 36 in 2025 [legislation-gov-uk/2026_public-general-acts-by-year]. Ministers made 1,120 to 1,139 statutory instruments a year [legislation-gov-uk/2026_statutory-instruments-by-year].

**Attention.** If 4 billion terrans each gave 30 minutes to one question, that would take about 250 million person-days. (Estimate: 4 × 10⁹ × 0.5 h ÷ 8 h.) A 450-member body sitting 200 days uses 90,000 member-days a year (estimate).

**Design implication:** About 40 Acts a year at one or two per body means 20–40 parallel bodies, or a shorter planetary list (estimate). Delegated rules need sampled review, not a jury each. Scope (VII.4) drives cost and speed more than pay does.

## Design patterns that work

- **Standing council, short-lived assemblies** (Ostbelgien). Works when parliament must answer and the council tracks follow-up. Fails on council skew, low public awareness, and workload above about one assembly a year.
- **Mixed citizen–MP committees** (Brussels). The MPs who deliberated push follow-up; small groups limit domination. Fails where citizens' votes stay consultative and nobody counts outcomes.
- **Witnessed, checkable lotteries with fair algorithms** (Paris, LEXIMIN). Needs published code, pools and quotas. Self-selection survives it.
- **A duty to answer every recommendation, with a dated review** (Ostbelgien, Brussels, Paris, the EU). Fails when the answering body grades itself and adopts what it already wanted.
- **Split roles** (Bouricius). Untested as a whole; the cost is coordination.
- **Online small groups with automated moderation** (Stanford; the *Science* study). Thousands deliberate at once, without hundreds of moderators. Untested for binding decisions, mixed-language groups or people offline.
- **Language-grouped tables with interpreted plenaries** (Stanford, the Conference). Keeps discussion fluent. Fails when small languages are dropped, or interpreters double as recruiters.
- **Staggered rotation with reserve lists** (Ostbelgien since 2023). Keeps experience while renewing members. Fails when successors come only from volunteering veterans.

## Tensions and open problems

- **Binding against acceptance.** Publics prefer advisory bodies, and Belgium's Council of State resists regulatory power for allotted councils.
- **Representative against voluntary.** Response runs 2–12%; compulsory service is proposed only for short juries.
- **Expertise against capture.** Lay members need drafters, yet in France drafters shaped most texts.
- **Equal pay against unequal incomes.** A flat US$600 and a proposed $500,000 a year bracket an untested planetary rate.
- **Coherence against overload.** Many single-issue bodies need a budget and coherence layer nobody has built.
- **Reach against equality.** Online formats cut cost but favour the connected and need a trusted platform.
- **Opposition.** Lot gives organised movements no home, while VIII.10 promises a right to organise opposition.
- **Not found.** EU panel costs, Brussels outcome counts, Paris after March 2026, and the full texts of Guerrero (2014) and Abizadeh (2021).

## Options for the next draft

- **VII.3 and VIII.4.** Replace the universal vote on every question with allotted issue legislatures that enact ordinary planetary law. Keep all-terran votes for Charter amendments (XI.3) and petitions to veto an enacted law.
- **VIII.2.** Keep the open map of opinion, and add an online deliberative poll for each act. Thousands drawn by lot would deliberate in machine-moderated groups by language, advising the legislature. Publish platform code and logs, and fund hosted access.
- **VIII.3.** Replace "drawn by lot to mirror humanity" with a stated method: frame, traits, algorithm, public verifiable lottery, minimum sizes, replacement rules and audit.
- **VIII.1 and VIII.3.** Add a standing Agenda Council by lot to sort initiatives, assign them, cap each body's load and track follow-through.
- **VIII.3–4.** Separate drafting from deciding, and attach minority reports to every output.
- **VIII.8.** Make each allotted body separate, with no dual seats. Add an allotted Oversight Council over staff, facilitators, interpreters and expert lists, and publish proceedings and every draft change.
- **VII.4.** List planetary competences, one issue body each, with a budget and coherence body above them.
- **Rotation and language.** Use staggered terms with reserve lists. Name exchange languages, group tables by language, and keep interpreters independent of recruiters.
- **Entrenchment.** Write the allotted bodies into the Charter, beyond any ordinary majority's reach.

## Design sketch: a planetary legislature by lot

| Body | Size | Term and rotation | Selection | Job |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Agenda Council | 300 | Three years; a sixth replaced every six months | Lot among former members, with quotas for youth, education and region | Sorts initiatives, assigns bodies, sets loads, tracks follow-up |
| Issue legislatures, one per listed competence (say 15) | 450 each | Three years; 75 replaced every six months, with reserves drawn at the same time | Lot from all terrans, stratified by world region, gender, age, education and language group | Hear evidence, consult, draft and pass ordinary law; two in-person sessions a year, online work between |
| Online deliberative poll | Several thousand per act | One weekend | Fresh lot, grouped by language | Machine-moderated small groups advise the legislature |
| Policy juries | 1,000 or more per act | Two or three days | Fresh lot from a standing pool | Hear both sides, then vote by secret ballot without debate |
| Rules and Oversight Councils | 150 each | Two years, staggered | Lot among former members | Set selection and petition rules; oversee staff, platforms and interpreters |
| All terrans | Everyone | n/a | n/a | Vote on Charter amendments and petition vetoes |

**Nesting and speed.** Community assemblies govern community matters (VII.4). Legislature and jury seats come straight from the planetary register, so volunteer skew does not compound; the councils draw veterans under quotas. A standing jury pool, drawn yearly as in Athens, could confirm or void the Stewards' emergency acts within days; unconfirmed acts lapse.

**Cost (estimates).** Fifteen bodies of 450 make 6,750 members. At 200 sitting days and €736–€1,861 per member-day (Madrid's per-session to France's per-day cost), they cost €1.0–2.5 billion a year: €0.12–0.31 per terran. Guerrero's proposed $500,000 salary would cost $3.4 billion alone (6,750 × $500,000). Juries for 50 acts a year (50 × 1,000 × 2 days) add €74–186 million. Replacing 2,250 members a year at Ostbelgien's to Brussels's 67–222 letters per seat means 150,000–500,000 invitations.

**Throughput and reach.** Fifteen bodies passing two to four acts a year yield 30–60 acts, close to the UK's 25–57 Acts a year in 2023–2025. About 52,250 seats would open each year, counting juries. So about one terran in 1,950 would serve in a lifetime (estimate: 8.14 billion ÷ (52,250 × 80)).

**Failure modes.** Falling response and attrition. Framing by organisers and expert lists. Recruiters who also translate and pay. Bribery of members, incoherence across bodies, and platform capture or outages. Repeal by an ordinary majority. One lottery operator would be a single point of failure, so use several, with open code.
