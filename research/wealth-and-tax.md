# The culture of enough: wealth limits and taxes

*Research note · Charter Part IV and open question "The numbers" · first pass, 10 October 2026 · compiled by Claude, not yet reviewed by Kara*

The $10 million limit matches Ingrid Robeyns' "political limit" in size, but no country has enacted a hard cap. The "fair burden" cap (IV.6) is the weakest clause; in France, a similar cap cut the richest payers' effective rate to 0.1%. A rough estimate puts about 2.9 million adults above $10 million, with about $80 trillion above the line. The limit starts at stage two (XII.3), with up to half of humanity outside, so flight and hidden assets remain live risks.

## What the Charter says

- **Norms (IV.1–IV.3).** Greed is hoarding while others fall below the floor or the planet passes its ceiling. It meets disapproval; comfort and savings are allowed.
- **Limit and climb (IV.4–IV.5).** No terran may hold more than $10 million; the excess passes to the Commons Fund each year. The first $2 million is untaxed. The yearly tax "starts at 1% and rises toward the limit", with rates set by vote.
- **Fair burden, shares, reporting (IV.6–IV.8).** Below the limit, combined taxes never exceed what income and assets earn in a year. Owners may pay in shares. Holdings through companies, trusts or relatives count as the owner's.
- **Commons and timing (IV.9, XII.3).** Air, water, land and knowledge are commons held by the Commons Fund. Amounts are 2026 US dollars, price-indexed. The limit applies from stage two, and members may leave until stage three (XII.5).

## Robeyns' limitarianism and the $10 million figure

Robeyns holds that no one should keep "surplus money" beyond what a fully flourishing life needs [robeyns/2019_extreme-wealth]. Her 2017 chapter sets a "riches line" in money, with an illustrative €200,000 a year [robeyns/2017_having-too-much]. She defends limitarianism as a political doctrine: the state taxes surplus away or reshapes institutions [robeyns/2017_having-too-much]. Governments can also build the norm by publicly praising giving [robeyns/2017_having-too-much].

In 2019 she wrote that, as fiscal policy, "limitarianism equals a top marginal tax rate of 100%" [robeyns/2019_extreme-wealth]. She cited a consensus revenue-maximizing top rate of about 70%. Only her political-equality argument holds the line at 100% [robeyns/2019_extreme-wealth].

Her 2024 book advocates "a political limit of roughly ten million per person", currency immaterial [gordon/2024_review-limitarianism]. The publisher adds that "no one needs more than one million" [robeyns/2024_limitarianism]. She ties €10 million to the Dutch context: "if you live in New York and don't have a pension, it should be more" [uu/2023_what-is-limitarianism]. Her ethical limit is €1 million per person [uu/2023_what-is-limitarianism]. She would limit inheritance to €200,000 [vrt/2023_robeyns-limit-wealth]. At the ECB rate of 9 October 2026, €10 million is about $11.2 million [ecb/2026_euro-reference-rates].

Dutch respondents place a riches line between €1 million and €3 million, whatever their own income [robeyns-et-al/2021_riches-line]. Most oppose enforced limits but back higher taxes on the super-rich to help the vulnerable [robeyns-et-al/2021_riches-line].

Differs from the drafting-session research: its €2.2 million reference point is the survey mean for a family of four, not a per-person figure. It also excludes pension wealth [robeyns/2021_too-rich-blog].

**For the Charter:** IV.4 keeps Robeyns' magnitude and per-person unit, but hardens her policy goal into a yearly 100% transfer. It omits her inheritance limit, and the Charter has no rule on gifts or bequests. Her case for 100% rests on political equality, which Part V.2 shares; the draft could say so.

## Wealth taxes in practice

The OECD counted 12 member countries with net wealth taxes in 1990 and 4 in 2017 [oecd/2018_net-wealth-taxes]. Repeals cited efficiency costs, capital flight, narrow bases, avoidance and high running costs [oecd/2018_net-wealth-taxes]. In 2016 they raised 0.2% (Spain) to 1.0% (Switzerland) of GDP [oecd/2018_net-wealth-taxes]. The OECD found them "more distortive and less equitable" than capital-income taxes, since they ignore actual returns [oecd/2018_net-wealth-taxes]. It still advised high thresholds, low rates, market values, instalments and third-party reporting [oecd/2018_net-wealth-taxes].

**Norway.** Finance Ministry data show 82 wealthy Norwegians, with NOK 46 billion ($4.3 billion), left in 2022–2023 [fortune/2024_norwegians-switzerland]. More than 70 went to Switzerland [fortune/2024_norwegians-switzerland]. Revenue still rose: Civita estimates ownership taxes at NOK 65 billion in 2023, against 38 billion in 2021 [fortune/2024_norwegians-switzerland].

**Spain.** The 60% cap leaves long-held gains out of income and gives no relief for tax on assets yielding no income [spain/1991_ley-19-1991-patrimonio]. The solidarity tax raised roughly €630 million in its first year and €35 million in its second [guiafiscal/2026_itsgf]. Regions such as Madrid and Andalusia reinstated their own wealth tax [guiafiscal/2026_itsgf], which is deductible from the state tax [spain/2022_ley-38-2022]. Wealth-tax receipts, which go to the regions, rose €682 million to €1,911 million [vozpopuli/2024_patrimonio-recaudacion]. The state tax worked by pulling regions up.

**France.** The 75% cap cut the effective ISF rate of the richest 0.001% to 0.1% of wealth [actu-juridique/2023_bilan-fiscalite-capital]. Of payers declaring €100–200 million, 84% were capped, reporting income of 0.2% of wealth [actu-juridique/2023_bilan-fiscalite-capital]. The cap cost about €2 billion [actu-juridique/2023_bilan-fiscalite-capital]. In 2012 the Constitutional Council required a cap, or an equivalent, for pre-2012 ISF rates [france/2012_cc-2012-654-dc]. Departures fell from about 950 a year (2011–2016) to 260 (2018–2021), against a departure rate near 0.2% [actu-juridique/2023_bilan-fiscalite-capital] [france-strategie/2023_capital-tax-evaluation].

**Germany.** In 1995 the Constitutional Court found the tax unequal. Real estate was valued at 1964/74 values, other assets at current values [germany/1995_bverfge-93-121]. The deadline for reform, 31 December 1996, passed unmet [germany/1995_bverfge-93-121] [oecd/2018_net-wealth-taxes]. Its dictum said total tax on expected yield should stay "in der Nähe einer hälftigen Teilung" (near an even split) [germany/1995_bverfge-93-121]. In 2006 the Court said this sets no binding ceiling [germany/2006_bverfge-115-97].

**Denmark.** After the 1989 cut, the very wealthy's taxable wealth rose about 30% within eight years; the moderately wealthy's, about 10% [jakobsen-et-al/2020_wealth-taxation-denmark]. Simulated 30-year effects were 20% and 70%, the latter an upper bound including avoidance [jakobsen-et-al/2020_wealth-taxation-denmark]. The 78% ceiling set the marginal wealth tax to zero for households it bound [jakobsen-et-al/2020_wealth-taxation-denmark].

**Caps.** The OECD found caps "create significant opportunities for tax avoidance", because taxpayers shrink reported income [oecd/2018_net-wealth-taxes]. The UK Wealth Tax Commission wrote: "We therefore firmly reject any suggestion of an income-based cap on liability" [wtc/2020_final-report].

Differs from the drafting-session research: it recommends capping combined tax at what assets earn. The OECD and the UK commission found such caps invite avoidance [oecd/2018_net-wealth-taxes] [wtc/2020_final-report].

Differs from the drafting-session research: Spain's state default scale is 0.2–3.5%, not 0.16–3.5% [spain/1991_ley-19-1991-patrimonio]. The OECD gives 2016 revenue as 0.2–1.0% of GDP, not 0.2–1.2% [oecd/2018_net-wealth-taxes]. Regional scales and later years were not checked.

**For the Charter:** The UK commission called a 1% yearly rate "high by international standards" [wtc/2020_final-report]. The Charter starts there, with a threshold ten times Norway's. Its 100% cap binds later than France's 75% or Spain's 60%, so it protects taxpayers less. With no floor, it can erase the tax where income is low.

## Global coordination

Gabriel Zucman's 2024 report for the Brazilian G20 presidency proposed a minimum tax of 2% of wealth on billionaires [zucman/2024_g20-blueprint]. It estimated $200–250 billion a year from about 3,000 people [zucman/2024_g20-blueprint]. Billionaires paid about 0.3% of their wealth in tax, against real pre-tax returns of about 7.5% [zucman/2024_g20-blueprint]. About half their wealth sits in private firms, to be valued by multiples of listed peers [zucman/2024_g20-blueprint].

The G20 finance ministers' Rio declaration of 26 July 2024 stayed soft. It reads: "With full respect to tax sovereignty, we will seek to engage cooperatively to ensure that ultra-high-net-worth individuals are effectively taxed" [g20/2024_rio-tax-declaration].

France's version, a 2% floor above €100 million, passed the National Assembly on 20 February 2025 [wikipedia/2026_taxe-zucman]. The Senate rejected it in June, and the Assembly rejected a budget amendment on 31 October 2025 [wikipedia/2026_taxe-zucman]. The 2026 budget, enacted on 20 February 2026, did not include it [rothschild/2026_loi-de-finances-2026] [wikipedia/2026_taxe-zucman]. It instead taxes yachts, aircraft and similar assets in family holding companies at 20% [rothschild/2026_loi-de-finances-2026]. The 2027 bill was presented on 1 October 2026; Assembly debate starts 13 October [deloitte/2026_plf-2027]. As of 10 October 2026, France has no minimum tax on large fortunes.

California votes on 3 November 2026 on Proposition 40, a one-time 5% tax on billionaires resident on 1 January 2026 [california/2026_proposition-40]. Payment may be spread over five years. The state's analyst expects some billionaires to leave, costing under $1 billion a year in income tax [california/2026_proposition-40].

Differs from the drafting-session research (update): since France's October 2025 vote, the 2026 budget passed without the tax. The 2027 bill is now in debate [rothschild/2026_loi-de-finances-2026] [deloitte/2026_plf-2027].

Differs from the drafting-session research: it says one planetary jurisdiction removes emigration and havens. Under XII.3 the limit starts with up to half of humanity outside, so departures remain possible until stage three [fortune/2024_norwegians-switzerland] [california/2026_proposition-40].

**For the Charter:** Coordination proposals target billionaires with minimum taxes, not multimillionaires with caps. The Charter's limit bites at 1/100 of Zucman's threshold. California's residency date, set before the vote, is one anti-flight tool.

## Paying in shares, and models for a Commons Fund

The UK Wealth Tax Commission recommended five-year instalments, pension deferral and a statutory deferral scheme [wtc/2020_final-report]. A text search of its final report found no proposal for payment in shares. For a one-off tax paid at 1% a year, 570,000 people would be cash-constrained at a £500,000 threshold; 65,000 at £2 million [wtc/2020_final-report]. One in five of them held business assets as their main illiquid asset [wtc/2020_final-report]. An annual 1% tax would lose 7–17% of its base to avoidance [wtc/2020_final-report].

In 2025 Zucman suggested that founders of unprofitable start-ups pay in shares to the state [lesechos/2025_zucman-impot-en-nature]. Spain's solidarity tax accepts listed cultural-heritage goods as payment [spain/2022_ley-38-2022].

**For the Charter:** Paying in shares solves cash shortages but not valuation, since brackets need a total value. The Commons Fund would accumulate stakes in every firm whose founder hits the limit. Norway shows a minority, diversified owner under a fiscal rule; the Charter sets no voting, sell-down or spending rules.

## Nothing hidden: registers and reporting (IV.8)

EU law required central beneficial-ownership registers from 2015 and opened them to the public in 2018 [eutaxobs/2023_global-tax-evasion-report] [ec/2026_aml-eu-level]. On 22 November 2022 the EU Court of Justice struck down public access [cjeu/2022_wm-sovim]. It found the interference "neither limited to what is strictly necessary nor proportionate" [cjeu/2022_wm-sovim]. It did not fault the earlier legitimate-interest regime [cjeu/2022_wm-sovim]. At least seven states then closed their registers to the public [eutaxobs/2023_global-tax-evasion-report]. The 2024 package applies from 2027 [ec/2026_aml-eu-level]; as proposed, it aimed to secure access for people with a legitimate interest [eutaxobs/2023_global-tax-evasion-report]. EUR-Lex blocked automated access, so the adopted text was not checked.

Automatic exchange of bank data reached "over 100 jurisdictions" by 2024 [g20/2024_rio-tax-declaration]. It cut offshore evasion about threefold, but about 25% of $12 trillion held offshore still escapes tax [eutaxobs/2023_global-tax-evasion-report]. The United States stays outside the standard, and real estate is not covered [eutaxobs/2023_global-tax-evasion-report]. Only 66 of 227 Norwegian residents owning Dubai property in 2020 reported it [eutaxobs/2023_global-tax-evasion-report]. The Observatory and ICRICT propose linking registers into a global asset registry; Zucman expects that to take decades [eutaxobs/2023_global-tax-evasion-report] [icrict/2022_global-asset-registry].

The United States has moved the other way. FinCEN's final rule, effective 14 August 2026, exempts US companies and US persons from ownership reporting [fincen/2026_boi-final-rule]. France's 2026 holding tax counts holdings of partners, ascendants, descendants and siblings [rothschild/2026_loi-de-finances-2026].

**For the Charter:** Courts have accepted reporting to authorities; public disclosure is where the CJEU pushed back [cjeu/2022_wm-sovim]. VII.6 ("transparency scales with power") would need a legitimate-interest test. "Relatives" needs a definition, such as France's family circle.

## The commons and Ostrom (IV.9)

The publisher's description of Ostrom's book notes that "neither the state nor the market" has been uniformly successful with shared resources [ostrom/1990_governing-the-commons]. Her design principles for lasting commons are clear boundaries, locally fitted rules, user participation and accountable monitoring [ostrom/2009_beyond-markets-and-states]. They continue with graduated sanctions, cheap local dispute resolution, recognized self-organization, and nested layers for large systems [ostrom/2009_beyond-markets-and-states]. About two-thirds of over 100 studies confirmed them [ostrom/2009_beyond-markets-and-states]. In 163 forests, government-protected ones were no denser than others; fit and legitimacy mattered more [ostrom/2009_beyond-markets-and-states]. For knowledge, the threats are intellectual-property law, overpatenting, licensing and overpricing [hess-ostrom/2006_knowledge-as-a-commons].

**For the Charter:** One planetary fund that "holds" air, water and land cuts against the principles on local rules, participation, self-organization and nesting. Ostrom's work points to users governing local commons, with the planetary layer setting ceilings. IV.9 also merges natural commons with a financial fund fed by taxes; they need different rules.

## The culture of enough (IV.1–IV.3)

In 50 experiments, incentives often crowded out social motives, sometimes crowded them in, and well-designed fines could complement them [bowles-polania-reyes/2012_incentives-social-preferences]. Bowles' book develops this argument; its publisher page could not be opened [bowles/2016_moral-economy]. Robert Frank argues people build bigger houses "mainly because that's what friends and neighbors do" [frank/2020_under-the-influence]. When Norway put tax records online in 2001, the happiness gap between richer and poorer people grew 29% [perez-truglia/2020_income-transparency-well-being]. Robeyns suggests studying non-monetary incentives such as esteem or honour [robeyns/2019_extreme-wealth].

The top 10% of households by emissions produce 34–45% of household emissions [ipcc/2022_ar6-wg3-spm]. The IPCC adds: "Addressing inequality and many forms of status consumption and focusing on wellbeing supports climate change mitigation efforts" [ipcc/2022_ar6-wg3-spm]. Beyond a threshold, more consumption is not closely tied to human progress [ipcc/2022_ar6-wg3-ch5].

**For the Charter:** Visibility can feed status contests rather than shame. A wealth cap also limits saving, not spending; near the limit, a yacht becomes cheaper than investing. That cuts against IV.3 and the ceiling.

**Rough estimate (calculation in this note, not a forecast).**

1. UBS millionaires average about $4.3–4.4 million, which fits a Pareto tail with α ≈ 1.30.
2. The 7.0 million with $5–100 million and 713,626 above $30 million imply α ≈ 1.28 from $5 to 30 million.
3. Adults above $10 million ≈ 7.05 million × 2^−1.28 ≈ **2.9 million** (2.6–3.2 million). That is about 0.08% of adults in UBS's sample.
4. Wealth above the line sums each fortune minus $10 million:
   - $10–30 million: about $27 trillion.
   - $30 million–$1 billion, with α ≈ 1.52 (fitting about 3,400 billionaires): about $34 trillion.
   - Above $1 billion: $20.1 trillion minus 3,428 × $1 billion ≈ $17 trillion.
5. Total ≈ **$79 trillion** (range $75–105 trillion), or 15–20% of household wealth in UBS's sample.
6. About 23 million adults (0.6%) hold over $2 million. Their wealth between $2 and 10 million totals $55–64 trillion.

The method mixes three sources with different methods; treat results as orders of magnitude.

**How IV.6 interacts.** Assume no wages, returns taxed as income, and an illustrative 40% income tax. The cap then binds once the wealth tax exceeds 60% of the return. Schedule A never binds in this grid; Schedule B binds at $9 million with 3% or 5% returns. A glide path reaching 100% at the limit would take $586,875 from a $5 million fortune. That is 1.7 to 3.9 times its yearly return, so the cap would override any such schedule above about $4 million.

The meaning of "earn" decides the outcome. Take a founder with $9 million in non-dividend shares and a $100,000 salary. If "earn" means realized income, all taxes stop near $100,000, below Schedule A's $131,250 wealth tax. If it means accrued returns of 5%, the cap is $550,000 and does not bind. At the cap, each extra dollar of income adds a dollar of tax: a 100% marginal rate.

## Tensions and open problems

- **IV.6 invites the French outcome.** Where realized income is low, the cap can erase the tax; in France the richest paid 0.1% of wealth [actu-juridique/2023_bilan-fiscalite-capital]. It also sets a 100% marginal rate at the cap.
- **A cliff, not a climb.** IV.5 fixes the 1% start but leaves the slope to a vote. A majority could keep 1% up to the 100% limit.
- **Volatility.** A yearly 100% transfer takes gains above the line but leaves losses with the owner. That penalizes risk and rewards undervaluation. Pay is averaged over years (V.3); wealth is not.
- **Valuation.** About half of billionaire wealth is in private firms [zucman/2024_g20-blueprint]. Germany's tax fell over unequal valuation [germany/1995_bverfge-93-121].
- **Stage-two flight and inner competition.** Members may leave until stage three (XII.5). Norway lost 82 wealthy residents in two years; France lost about 0.2% of payers a year [fortune/2024_norwegians-switzerland] [actu-juridique/2023_bilan-fiscalite-capital]. Swiss cantons competed on rates [oecd/2018_net-wealth-taxes].
- **Family and bequests.** A per-person limit invites splitting wealth among relatives. Counting relatives' holdings as the owner's could sweep in adult children's own wealth.
- **The Commons Fund has no rules** for spending, voting received shares, or ethics. Norway paused new ethics exclusions in November 2025 [nbim/2026_exclusion-of-companies].
- **Public support and consumption.** Dutch respondents back taxes but not caps [robeyns-et-al/2021_riches-line]. A cap leaves luxury consumption untouched, while top emitters drive 34–45% of household emissions [ipcc/2022_ar6-wg3-spm].
- **Currency.** UBS links Japan's stagnant count of $5–100 million holders to the yen's fall [ubs/2026_global-wealth-report]. A dollar limit would move with exchange rates.
- **Attribution.** Robeyns frames €10 million as a Dutch-context policy goal, paired with an inheritance limit [uu/2023_what-is-limitarianism] [vrt/2023_robeyns-limit-wealth]. "Adapts" fits better than "draws on".

## Options for the next draft

- **Replace the income-based cap** with deferral and instalments [wtc/2020_final-report], or a cap against a presumed yield, as in Germany's dictum [germany/1995_bverfge-93-121]. If a cap stays, add Spain's 20% floor and exclusions [spain/1991_ley-19-1991-patrimonio].
- **Write the glide path into the Charter,** and measure wealth as a three-to-five-year average, as V.3 does for pay.
- **Settle share payments by fraction.** Paying 1% of shares for a 1% rate avoids pricing them; only brackets need values, for example from listed-peer multiples [zucman/2024_g20-blueprint].
- **Give the Commons Fund a charter:** protected principal, a real-return spending rule, an independent board and published ethics rules [apfc/2026_history] [nbim/2026_about-the-fund]. Decide who votes its shares.
- **Split IV.9.** Govern natural commons in nested, user-run layers [ostrom/2009_beyond-markets-and-states]; keep the financial fund separate.
- **Add inheritance and gift rules,** for example Robeyns' €200,000 limit [vrt/2023_robeyns-limit-wealth]. Define "relatives" with a family-circle test [rothschild/2026_loi-de-finances-2026].
- **Tier transparency:** full reporting to authorities, public access by legitimate interest [cjeu/2022_wm-sovim].
- **Plan for stage-two flight:** residency look-back dates [california/2026_proposition-40], exit taxes and a "tax collector of last resort" [zucman/2024_g20-blueprint].
- **Index the limit to purchasing power,** not market exchange rates, and pair it with a tax on status consumption [ipcc/2022_ar6-wg3-spm].
