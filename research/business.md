# Business: personhood, pay, ownership and voice

*Research note · Charter Part V and the open question "The numbers" (pay limits) · first pass, 10 October 2026 · compiled by Claude, not yet reviewed by Kara*

Part V has real precedents, but its wording goes further than any democracy I found. It would also bind allies, since newspapers, unions, NGOs and campaign groups are all "organizations". The 100x pay rule is in effect a hard cap. Israel's 35:1 cap for financial firms cut pay without hurting stock returns; Swiss voters rejected 12:1 by 65.3%. Two phrases need fixing: "lowest full-time pay" invites part-time substitution, and start-up money is one barrier to cooperatives among several.

## What the Charter says

- **V.1** Organizations, including nonprofits, keep property, contracts and the power to sue and be sued, but "hold no political rights".
- **V.2** No organization may spend to influence "a vote or a public decision". Individual spending is capped, and "the press stays free".
- **V.3** Pay above 50 times the lowest full-time pay is taxed steeply; above 100 times it goes to the Commons Fund. The base counts contractors and outsourced workers; the top counts stock, averaged over several years.
- **V.4** Workers earn a growing ownership share, and long-term workers become voting members. The Commons Fund helps cooperatives start, "since a lack of start-up money is a main reason so few exist".
- **V.5** In "larger organizations", workers elect at least a third of the board.
- Credits: Mondragon, employee-ownership plans, German codetermination. Part IV.7 (wealth tax paid in shares to the Commons Fund) bears on V.4.

## Organizations are not persons (V.1)

US corporate personhood had a thin start. The syllabus of *Santa Clara* (1886) says corporations are persons under the Fourteenth Amendment. The opinion itself turned on how fences were taxed. It found it "not necessary to consider any other questions". The reporter recorded Chief Justice Waite telling counsel the Court was "all of opinion that it does" [us-supreme-court/1886_santa-clara-v-southern-pacific]. Justice Rehnquist later said this was settled "with neither argument nor discussion" [us-supreme-court/1978_first-national-bank-v-bellotti].

*Bellotti* (26 April 1978, 5–4) struck down a Massachusetts criminal ban on corporate spending on ballot questions. Two banks and three companies wanted to oppose a 1976 amendment allowing a graduated income tax. The majority held that the worth of speech "does not depend upon the identity of its source". Chief Justice Burger found no factual line between media and other corporations [us-supreme-court/1978_first-national-bank-v-bellotti]. *Citizens United* (21 January 2010, 5–4) overruled *Austin* and struck the federal ban on corporate and union independent spending. It upheld disclosure rules. The plaintiff was a nonprofit distributing a film. Justice Stevens wrote that corporations are "not themselves members of 'We the People'" [us-supreme-court/2010_citizens-united-v-fec].

Move to Amend's text limits constitutional rights to "natural persons only". It leaves organizations' privileges to ordinary law, orders governments to regulate or ban contributions and spending, and protects the press [move-to-amend/2025_we-the-people-amendment]. H.J.Res. 54 was referred to the House Judiciary Committee on 12 February 2025. As of 10 October 2026 it had 82 cosponsors and no further action [us-congress/2025_hjres-54].

Other democracies give organizations rights and limit their money. Germany applies basic rights to domestic legal persons "to the extent that the nature of such rights permits" [germany/1949_grundgesetz]. The European Convention protects "every natural or legal person" in their possessions and lets NGOs bring claims [coe/1950_echr].

**For the Charter:** The critique of US doctrine stands up: personhood began in a syllabus, and the speech rulings were 5–4. But the democratic norm limits organizational money and keeps rights. "Political rights" needs a definition: donations, campaign spending, lobbying, petitions, litigation, publishing.

## No money in votes (V.2)

**Canada.** From 1 January 2007, contributions by corporations, unions and organizations were banned [canada-tbs/2006_reforming-political-party-financing]. Today only citizens and permanent residents may give (s. 363). The statute's limit is C$1,500 a year per party, rising C$25 each 1 January (s. 367). Corporations and groups such as unions may still spend as registered "third parties", from a base limit of C$350,000 per general election (s. 350) [canada/2000_canada-elections-act]. In *Harper* (2004) the Supreme Court upheld earlier third-party caps for "electoral fairness"; three judges dissented in part [scc/2004_harper-v-canada].

**France.** The law of 19 January 1995 banned donations and in-kind help from legal persons other than parties. Public funding and campaign spending caps go with the ban [transparency-france/2015_financement-vie-politique]. A person may give a candidate €4,600 per election and parties €7,500 a year [france-senat/2021_question-12087-l52-8]. Money found channels. Before a 2013 reform, micro-parties multiplied to pass funds to candidates [transparency-france/2015_financement-vie-politique]. Parties may fund candidates without limit, and in 2021 the ministry did not treat this as circumvention [france-senat/2021_question-12087-l52-8]. Enforcement bites slowly. Prosecutors said Sarkozy's 2012 campaign spent nearly €43 million against a €22.5 million cap; his conviction became final on 26 November 2025 [thelocal-fr/2025_sarkozy-bygmalion-final].

**United Kingdom.** PPERA lists UK companies and trade unions as permissible donors [uk/2000_ppera-section-54]. It caps spending instead. A party may spend £54,010 per constituency contested [uk/2000_ppera-schedule-9], about £34 million across Great Britain after an 80% rise in November 2023 [ifg/2024_election-spending-explainer]. Non-party campaigners in England may spend 2% of the maximum party limit [uk/2000_ppera-schedule-10]. In UK-wide referendums, the lead campaigner on each side may spend £5 million and other registered campaigners £500,000 [uk/2000_ppera-schedule-14]. Penalties are small: fines are capped at £20,000, and police referrals have never produced a prosecution. A February 2026 bill would raise the cap to £500,000 [ifg/2024_election-spending-explainer].

**Switzerland's** federal rules, in force since 2022, are disclosure rules. Campaigns spending over CHF 50,000 on a federal vote must disclose funding, and donors above CHF 15,000 are named [sfao/2026_political-funding-transparency-rules].

**Protecting the press.** The UK and Canada exempt content, not owners. UK rules for non-party campaigners exclude publishing "any matter relating to an election, other than an advertisement" in newspapers and broadcasts [uk/2000_ppera-schedule-8a]. Canada excludes "an editorial, a debate, a speech, an interview, a column, a letter, a commentary or news" from election advertising [canada/2000_canada-elections-act]. The US exempts media corporations, but *Bellotti* and *Citizens United* doubted that line can be drawn [us-supreme-court/1978_first-national-bank-v-bellotti] [us-supreme-court/2010_citizens-united-v-fec].

**What courts allow.** Limits must leave room to speak. Strasbourg found a £5 limit on one campaigner disproportionate (*Bowman*, 19 February 1998, 14–6) [echr/1998_bowman-v-uk]. It upheld, 9–8, the UK ban on paid political broadcast ads by campaigning bodies (*Animal Defenders*, 22 April 2013) [echr/2013_animal-defenders-v-uk].

**Evidence.** In Brazil, stricter spending limits in mayoral races drew more, and less wealthy, candidates and cut incumbents' advantage [avis/2017_campaign-spending-limits-brazil]. *Citizens United* is associated with a rise of about 4 percentage points in Republicans' chances in state house races [klumpp/2016_business-of-american-democracy]. One US bill would have required 75% of shareholders and directors to approve corporate spending over $10,000 per candidate [us-senate/2018_s3348-accountable-capitalism-act].

**For the Charter:** Banning corporate and union donations is mainstream. Banning all organizational spending on "a vote or a public decision" is not: NGOs, unions and campaign groups are registered and capped, not banned. "Public decision" also reaches lobbying, which needs its own rule. Since Part VIII decides by referendum, the closest models are UK referendum caps and Swiss disclosure. A content-based press exemption avoids deciding who counts as "the press".

## Pay stays proportionate (V.3) and "The numbers"

**Current US ratios.** EPI finds CEO pay at the top 350 US firms rose 14.0% in 2025, to $27.9 million on average. That is 325 times a typical worker on the realized measure and 247 times on the granted measure; in 1965 it was 21 times [epi/2026_ceo-pay-2025]. AFL-CIO finds S&P 500 CEOs averaged $22.8 million in 2025, excluding Elon Musk. Their average company ratio was 312:1, up from 285:1. With Musk's $158 billion Tesla package included, the average ratio was 5,387:1 [aflcio/2026_paywatch-12-things]. EPI excludes the package because it was "not paid and likely never will be" [epi/2026_ceo-pay-2025].

Differs from the drafting-session research: its "about 220 to 325 times" range omits the Musk effect. How a rule values unvested stock decides whether it bites.

**Counting rules and gaming.**

- *Contractors.* The SEC rule counts all employees worldwide, including part-time, temporary and seasonal staff. It excludes contractors and staff of unaffiliated firms. Firms may drop up to 5% of non-US staff, use sampling, and fix the median employee for three years [sec/2015_pay-ratio-rule]. Israel counts outsourced staff [bank-of-israel/2024_executive-pay-cap-law]. S. 2818 targets workforce changes "including by using the services of contractors rather than employees" [us-senate/2025_s2818-tax-excessive-ceo-pay]. The Swiss government warned firms might outsource tasks such as cleaning [switzerland/2012_botschaft-1-12].
- *Part-time.* Israel's evaluator names "hiring part-time rather than full-time employees if only the latter are considered in the ratio" as a dodge. Shortening vesting periods also lowers measured pay [bank-of-israel/2024_executive-pay-cap-law]. UK reporting uses full-time-equivalent pay at the 25th, 50th and 75th percentiles [uk/2018_si-2018-860-pay-ratio].
- *Substitution.* UK regulators dropped the bonus cap because fixed pay rose, which "reduces a firm's ability to adjust variable remuneration to absorb losses" [fca/2023_ps23-15-bonus-cap]. Counting total pay, as V.3 does, avoids this.
- *Averaging.* S. 2818 averages over five years. German law asks listed companies to set variable pay on a multi-year basis [germany/1965_aktiengesetz-87].
- *Structure.* Hutton found one multiple "would however be unfair, hitting some organisations more than others". A 20:1 cap would have allowed £270,000 at HMRC but £315,000 at the smaller Land Registry [hutton/2011_fair-pay-review].
- *Results.* After Israel's law, departures of top-five executives rose 80% (15 to 27 people). Stock returns were no worse and risk-taking did not change, but return on equity fell in the estimates. Some firms may have raised their lowest pay [bank-of-israel/2024_executive-pay-cap-law].

**US coverage may shrink.** On 19 May 2026 the SEC proposed ending pay-ratio disclosure for non-accelerated filers. Only large accelerated filers would report: 19.2% of reporting companies, against 35.4% classed that way today [sec/2026_release-33-11419]. Portland's surtax rests on those disclosures [portland/2026_city-code-7-02-500]. Differs from the drafting-session research (update): two Portland councilors proposed in September 2026 to raise the surtax. It would run from 25% at 50:1 to 500% at 500:1, raising a claimed $72 million a year [wweek/2026_portland-ceo-surtax-proposal].

**How 50x and 100x compare** (my arithmetic). A full-time job at the federal minimum of $7.25 an hour [us-dol/2026_federal-minimum-wage] pays $15,080 a year. Then 50x is $754,000 and 100x is $1,508,000. At $15 an hour the lines are $1.56 million and $3.12 million. Average 2025 S&P 500 CEO pay of $22.8 million is about 1,512 times the minimum-wage figure. At that pay, 100x binds wherever the lowest full-time pay is under $228,000. Binding caps run from 6:1 (Mondragon) to 35:1 (Israel). Tax schemes start at 50:1 or 100:1 of *median* pay, so V.3's 50x of *lowest* pay is stricter than S. 2818's 50x.

**For the Charter:** Counting contractors and stock and averaging over years all have precedents. The weak points are "full-time", the edge of "outsourced", how unvested stock is valued, who pays, and owners paid in dividends. A 100% rate above 100x is a hard cap. Switzerland rejected one and the UK dropped its bonus cap, while Israel's sector cap cut pay without hurting stock returns.

## Workers become owners (V.4)

**Mondragon.** The group began in 1956 and now has 81 self-governing cooperatives, about 70,000 people and 12 research centres [mondragon/2026_about-us]. Each member invests and has one vote in the general assembly [kasmir/2016_mondragon-successes-challenges]. Each cooperative's assembly sets its pay ratio [co-op-news/2025_co-operative-approaches-to-pay]. Two tiers formed after it went global in 1990. Its roughly 100 foreign subsidiaries are not worker-owned, and in 2016 "only one-third of its employees are members" [kasmir/2016_mondragon-successes-challenges].

Fagor Electrodomésticos failed in 2013. Sales had fallen 37% since 2007, and debt stood at €800 million [geo/2013_mondragon-cooperative-faltering] or €859 million [fortune/2013_fagor-workers-lock-in]. Mondragon had lent about €300 million, and workers had voted pay cuts of about 20% [fortune/2013_fagor-workers-lock-in]. The group's General Council called the rescue plan "not viable" [geo/2013_mondragon-cooperative-faltering]. Basque members retired early or moved to other cooperatives. Contract workers and 3,500 subsidiary staff "were not similarly protected" [kasmir/2016_mondragon-successes-challenges]. Members also leave. Ampo and Irizar left in 2008. Ulma and Orona voted to leave on 16 December 2022, taking 11,000 of 80,000 workers and ending 10% profit payments to a common fund [co-op-news/2023_ulma-orona-leave-mondragon].

**US ESOPs.** Louis Kelso devised the ESOP buyout in 1956 for Peninsula Newspapers, and ERISA (1974) wrote ESOPs into the tax code [kelso-institute/2026_important-dates]. ERISA defines an ESOP as a plan "designed to invest primarily in qualifying employer securities" [us/1974_erisa-section-407]. In 2023 there were 6,609 plans with 15.1 million participants. But 12.2 million sit in 511 public-company plans, and 6,098 private plans cover 2.9 million. The count was 6,669 in 2015 [nceo/2026_employee-ownership-by-the-numbers]. A trustee holds and votes the shares. In private firms employees direct votes only on a few matters, such as an asset sale [nceo/2026_how-an-esop-works]. Differs from the drafting-session research (nuance): its "15M participants" is right, but about four-fifths are in public-company plans.

**UK employee-ownership trusts.** From 2014, an owner selling a controlling stake to a trust for all employees on equal terms paid no capital gains tax. Tax-free bonuses up to £3,600 a year are allowed. The conditions require no employee vote [uk/2014_finance-act-schedule-37]. Since 30 October 2024, trustees must be UK-resident, and former owners and connected people must be under half of them. The price cannot exceed market value, and clawback runs four tax years [uk/2025_finance-act-schedule-6]. From 26 November 2025, only half the gain is relieved [uk/2026_finance-act-section-35]. The UK had 2,925 employee-owned businesses in September 2026, after 125 transitions so far that year [eoa/2026_uk-closes-in-on-3000]. I found no verified series showing whether the cut slowed transitions.

**Sweden's wage-earner funds.** Meidner's 1975 book became a 1976 union (LO) congress report. Its aims were solidaristic wages, less wealth concentration, and worker influence through co-ownership [sweden/1983_prop-1983-84-50]. I could not open Meidner's own text, so the 1976 share mechanism is unverified here [meidner/1978_employee-investment-funds]. The 1983 law created five regional funds inside the state pension system, each with nine government-appointed members, at least five representing employees. A 20% tax on real profits above an exemption, plus a 0.2-point pension levy, funded them. No fund could reach 8% of a listed company's shares or votes [sweden/1983_prop-1983-84-50]. Inflows stopped at the end of 1990 by design. In August 1991 the funds held SEK 22.4 billion. The Bildt government then wound them up, saying they clashed with the private saving the economy needed [sweden/1991_prop-1991-92-36]. Assessments differ. Westerberg finds they slightly missed their financial targets [westerberg/2022_wage-earner-funds-market-turn]. Pontusson and Kuruvilla find they met them but had little influence, given their size [pontusson/1992_swedish-wage-earner-funds]. Whyman calls the results mixed [whyman/2004_wage-earner-funds-myth-reality]. Capital owners fought them as an existential threat; most Social Democratic voters saw them as remote [warner/2025_asymmetric-mobilisation] [pontusson/1993_labor-initiated-reforms].

**Do cooperatives work?** In Uruguay, worker-managed firms had a 29% lower hazard of closing, linked to steadier employment [burdin/2014_worker-managed-firms-uruguay]. Pérotin's review finds worker cooperatives in most industries, often larger than other firms, surviving at least as well and probably more productive [perotin/2013_worker-cooperatives].

**Is start-up money "a main reason" so few exist?** Partly. Dow traces the rarity to labour being inalienable. Worker firms "face financing problems, encounter collective choice dilemmas, and have difficulty creating markets for control positions" [dow/2003_governing-the-firm]. Finance is one of three. Hansmann explains ownership forms by weighing market-contracting costs against ownership costs [hansmann/1996_ownership-of-enterprise]; I could not open his worker-ownership chapters. Pérotin says so few exist because "very few are created". She cites information and other entry barriers, weak law against demutualisation, and the pull of existing cooperatives [perotin/2013_worker-cooperatives]. Italy has a dedicated creation fund. Cooperatives in recognized federations pay 3% of annual profits into funds that may only promote and finance cooperatives [italy/1992_legge-59].

Differs from the drafting-session research: its link to Pérotin's report returned "404 Not Found" on 10 October 2026; this note uses her 2013 article.

**For the Charter:** V.4 goes beyond ESOPs and trusts by giving votes, as Mondragon does. Mondragon also shows the risks: non-member tiers, a flagship failure and exits. "Growing share" is undefined, and individual accounts and collective funds behave differently. The start-up claim should call finance one barrier among several.

## Workers have a voice (V.5)

**Germany.** The 1976 Codetermination Act covers companies with usually more than 2,000 employees, counted group-wide. Shareholders and employees get equal supervisory-board seats: 12, 16 or 20 [germany/1976_mitbestimmungsgesetz]. Without a two-thirds vote, the shareholder side picks the chair, who casts a second vote after a repeated tie [germany/1976_mitbestimmungsgesetz]. The 2004 One-Third Act gives employees a third of the seats above 500 employees [germany/2004_drittelbeteiligungsgesetz].

**Europe.** ETUI finds board-level employee representation across both sectors in 14 of 30 states, and only in parts of the public sector in five more. Thresholds run from 25 to 1,000 employees; seats run from one to half [etui/2026_board-level-representation-across-europe].

**Evidence.** Jäger, Schoefer and Heining use a 1994 German cutoff. A one-third mandate had no effect on wages, rent sharing or the labour share, and if anything raised investment [jager/2021_labor-in-the-boardroom]. I could not open Gorton and Schmid (2004), an older study of parity and firm value [gorton/2004_codetermination]. Rights must be used. In Denmark only 20% of eligible companies had employee board members in 2021 [etui/2025_denmark]. A 2020 Swedish union estimate put the shortage of employee board members at about 50,000 [etui/2026_sweden].

**For the Charter:** "At least a third" matches Germany's lower tier, not its parity tier. The draft needs a size threshold, a definition of "board", a tie-break rule, group-wide counting, and support so workers use the right.

## Tensions and open problems

- **Allies are organizations.** V.1–V.2 reach parties, unions, NGOs and groups backing a Part VIII proposal; only the press is carved out, and it is undefined. That sits uneasily with VIII.10's right to "organize peaceful opposition". Strasbourg rejects limits that act as a "total barrier" [echr/1998_bowman-v-uk].
- **"Public decision" reaches lobbying.** I found no precedent banning all organizational lobbying, and the draft has no lobbying rule.
- **Who is "the press"?** US justices doubted media and other corporations can be told apart. Content-based exemptions avoid this but need online definitions.
- **Money finds channels.** France shows pass-through via parties, micro-parties and double billing. UK fines top out at £20,000 [ifg/2024_election-spending-explainer].
- **"Full-time" is a loophole.** The drafting-session research recommended full-time-*equivalent* pay; the Charter dropped "equivalent". Israel's evaluator names part-time hiring as a dodge [bank-of-israel/2024_executive-pay-cap-law].
- **"Outsourced" has no edge.** Is a supplier's cleaner an outsourced worker of every client? Israel counted staff placed through outsourcing firms.
- **Stock valuation.** Realized and granted measures differ (325 vs 247 in 2025) [epi/2026_ceo-pay-2025]. One contingent grant lifted the S&P 500 average ratio to 5,387:1 [aflcio/2026_paywatch-12-things].
- **Ratios favour high-wage firms.** If the lowest full-timer earns $200,000, 100x allows $20 million. Hutton's HMRC example shows the same bias [hutton/2011_fair-pay-review].
- **Who pays.** V.3 does not say whether the person or the organization is taxed, or how working owners' dividends count. Israel counted management and consulting fees.
- **Part IV.** IV.6 caps combined taxes at a year's income and returns; a 100% slice could collide with it. IV.7 sends shares to the Commons Fund, and V.4 does not say whether workers get them.
- **The start-up claim** overstates finance [dow/2003_governing-the-firm] [perotin/2013_worker-cooperatives].
- **Membership tiers.** Only a third of Mondragon's workforce were members in 2016 [kasmir/2016_mondragon-successes-challenges]. V.4 does not define "long-term" or say whether foreign subsidiaries count.
- **Fragility.** Sweden scrapped its funds after eight years; the UK halved trust relief after eleven.
- **V.5 has no threshold.** Comparators run from 25 to 2,000 employees.

## Options for the next draft

- **Define "political rights"**: no campaign donations or paid campaign ads by organizations, and lobbying disclosed and capped. Keep expression, petition, litigation and publishing.
- **Add a civic-association category**: groups funded only by capped individual gifts may campaign within caps, like Canada's third parties.
- **Use a content-based press exemption** modelled on PPERA Schedule 8A or Canada's definition, with paid content labelled.
- **Pair caps with disclosure** (Swiss thresholds: CHF 50,000 per campaign, CHF 15,000 per donor) and with fines that deter.
- **Rewrite V.3's base** as "lowest full-time-equivalent pay, counting anyone working through a contractor, agency or franchise".
- **Define pay** as all value for work: salary, bonus, stock at vesting, fees via controlled companies, and working owners' dividends. Average over five years, as S. 2818 does.
- **Decide who pays.** A company surtax (Portland, S. 2818) is simpler; a personal 100% rate is closer to the intent.
- **Add an absolute ceiling** beside the ratio, or link top pay to the Part IV wealth limit, so high-wage firms cannot escape.
- **Reword V.4's claim**: "Start-up finance is one of several barriers, with decision costs, unsellable membership and weak law."
- **Fund new cooperatives as Italy does**: a share of cooperative profits flows to a development fund.
- **Define "long-term" and "growing share"**, for example membership within six years (the ESOP vesting maximum), with votes attached.
- **Set V.5's threshold**, for example one third above 50 or 500 employees (Norway, Germany), counted group-wide. Define "board" and the tie-break.
