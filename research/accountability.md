# Accountability: courts, enforcement and restorative justice

*Research note · Charter Part IX · first pass, 10 October 2026 · compiled by Claude, not yet reviewed by Kara*

The EU's money ladder worked slowly, and mainly through an election: Hungary lost €1.04 billion, but reformed only after Orbán lost in April 2026. Two clauses clash with existing privacy and victim-protection law: open ledgers of every enforcement action (IX.10) and open hearings with no exceptions (IX.4). Universal standing (IX.1) needs a filter, and no modern state lets an allotted body pick judges; Swiss voters rejected judges by lot in 2021. Norway, often cited as an unarmed model, has allowed routine police arming since June 2025.

## What the Charter says

Part IX opens a Planetary Court to "any terran", with community courts below it (IX.1). An assembly drawn by lot picks judges for one long term (IX.2), and inspectors answer to the Court (IX.3). Penalties fall on the people and institutions responsible, "never on whole communities" (IX.5). Enforcement moves from publishing findings to withholding funds to reclaiming assets, and only a court may order an arrest (IX.6). Punishment aims at repair, and confinement stays close to normal life (IX.8–IX.9). An Ombudsman oversees enforcers, and enforcement actions go on open ledgers unless a victim's privacy requires otherwise (IX.10).

## 1. The EU enforcement ladder (IX.6)

**Infringements and fines.** The Commission sends a reasoned opinion and can then take a state to the Court of Justice [eu/2016_tfeu-article-258]. The Court can fine a state that ignores a judgment, or one that fails to notify how it transposed a directive [eu/2016_tfeu-article-260]. Poland's €1,000,000-a-day penalty of 27 October 2021 came from interim-relief proceedings, not from Article 260 [cjeu/2021_poland-daily-penalty-order]. In April 2023 the Court halved it [cjeu/2023_poland-penalty-reduced]. Poland had abolished the Disciplinary Chamber but complied only in part.

**Budget conditionality.** Regulation 2020/2092 lets the Council, by qualified majority, suspend EU money when rule-of-law breaches affect the budget [eu/2020_regulation-2020-2092]. The state must keep paying final recipients (Art. 5(2)). Suspended commitments are lost if not restored within two years (Art. 7(3)). The Court of Justice upheld the regulation in February 2022 [cjeu/2022_conditionality-judgments]. In December 2022 the Council suspended 55% of commitments under three Hungarian cohesion programmes [eu/2022_council-implementing-decision-2022-2506]. That was about €6.3 billion; by January 2024 Hungary was still the only case [european-commission/2024_conditionality-report]. Reassessments in December 2023 and December 2024 kept the measures [european-commission/2026_conditionality-page]. €1.04 billion lapsed at the end of 2024, the first such loss for a member state [kyiv-independent/2025_hungary-loses-1bn].

**After the election.** Orbán lost the April 2026 election. In May the new prime minister, Péter Magyar, agreed reforms to unblock about €16.4 billion, including joining the EPPO [euronews/2026_hungary-unlocks-16bn]. On 23 September the Commission proposed lifting all conditionality measures [european-commission/2026_com-2026-516]. That would release €4.2 billion in cohesion funds [euobserver/2026_eu-unlock-4-2bn-hungary]. The Council's budget committee accepted the text unchanged on 1 October [council-eu/2026_ia-item-note-13880-26]. Formal adoption was still pending on 10 October 2026. I could not reconcile the €4.2 billion with the original €6.3 billion and the €1.04 billion lost.

**Collateral harm.** CEPS found the shield for final recipients "largely ineffective" [ceps/2026_orban-defeat-conditionality]. No recipient had a claim yet, because the suspension came before the programmes began. CEPS also cites evidence that the government steered other funds away from non-aligned beneficiaries such as Budapest.

**Article 7 TEU.** A "clear risk" finding needs four-fifths of the Council, and a "serious and persistent breach" needs unanimity [eu/2016_teu-article-7]. Hungary's procedure, opened in 2018, is still open [hungarian-helsinki-committee/2026_rule-of-law-file].

Differs from the drafting-session research: it says the EU "froze about €35–36B" for Hungary. By April 2026 about €18 billion was still withheld: €10.4 billion in recovery funds and €7.4 billion in cohesion funds [ceps/2026_orban-defeat-conditionality]. From May to September 2026 almost all of it was being released [euronews/2026_hungary-unlocks-16bn] [european-commission/2026_com-2026-516]. The figure needs a date and an outcome.

**For the Charter:** The EU already runs a version of "publish, withhold, reclaim". Its annual Rule of Law Report publishes, apart from the money tool [european-commission/2026_conditionality-page]. Three features worked: qualified-majority voting, a court that upheld the tool, and automatic lapse. Two failed: over three years of withholding did not move the government, and the shield for innocent recipients did not hold. Leverage paid off only once voters changed the government.

## 2. Standing: who may bring a case (IX.1)

**Colombia.** Article 86 of the 1991 Constitution lets any person ask any judge for a tutela to protect fundamental rights [colombia/1991_constitution-art-86]. Judges must decide within ten days. Every ruling goes to the Constitutional Court, which may select it for review. People filed 923,397 tutelas in 2025, according to Court data reported in the press [minuto60/2026_tutelas-record]. That is 17.4 per 1,000 people, and 34% concern health. Use ranged from 2.55 per 1,000 in Vichada to 31.12 in San Andrés. Landau found that most social-rights enforcement "has benefitted middle- or upper-class groups, rather than the poor" [landau/2012_reality-social-rights-enforcement].

**India.** From the late 1970s the Supreme Court loosened standing to hear public interest litigation [gauri/2009_pil-india]. Yet in 1997–2007 it took about 260 such cases a year, about 0.4% of its docket. Of the tens of thousands of letters it receives each year, only a handful become cases. Win rates were higher for advantaged claimants, reversing the 1980s pattern.

**Hungary.** Until 2012 anybody could ask the Constitutional Court to review any rule, with no time limit and no personal stake. The Court's president later said this "resulted in an unmanageable workload" [hungary-constitutional-court/2017_sulyok-presentation]. The Venice Commission called the actio popularis "not requisite in a democratic state ruled by law" [venice-commission/2012_opinion-hungary-constitutional-court].

**For the Charter:** Open standing works only with fast lower courts and a top court that picks its cases. At Colombia's rate, a population of 100 million would file about 1.7 million cases a year. Open doors also favour people with lawyers. The draft does not say who filters cases or how poor claimants get help.

## 3. Choosing judges and their terms (IX.2)

**Lot.** Athens filled its jury-courts by lot from citizens over thirty who were not in debt to the treasury [aristotle/1952_athenaion-politeia]. Lot picked the jurors who decided cases, not people who chose judges. Switzerland's 2021 Justiz-Initiative is the closest modern design to IX.2 [switzerland/2021_foj-justiz-initiative]. An expert commission would admit candidates, and lot would pick federal judges to serve until five years past retirement age. Voters rejected it by 2,335,148 to 1,094,989, or 68.1%, and every canton said no [switzerland/2022_federal-council-decree-vote-results]. Mexico's 2024 reform elects judges, after a public lottery cuts each list of four rated candidates to two [mexico/2026_constitution-arts-94-96]. Turnout in the first judicial election, in June 2025, was an estimated 12.57–13.32% [efe/2025_mexico-judicial-election-turnout].

**Lay selectors.** England and Wales's Judicial Appointments Commission must have a lay chair [uk/2005_constitutional-reform-act-sch-12]. Its lay commissioners are appointed for fixed terms, not drawn by lot [jac/2026_commissioners]. I found no modern case of an allotted body choosing judges.

**Terms.** German constitutional judges serve 12 years, retire at 68, stay until replaced and cannot be re-elected [germany/1951_bverfgg]. A 20 December 2024 amendment wrote these rules into the Basic Law [buzer/2024_gg-article-93-versions]. Article 93 now also allows a law letting one electing chamber step in if the other deadlocks [germany/2024_grundgesetz-art-93]. The bill's sponsors cited efforts in some European countries to question constitutional courts' independence [bundestag/2024_drucksache-20-12977]. Strasbourg judges serve nine years without re-election, and only two-thirds of their peers can dismiss one [coe/1950_echr]. The Earth Constitution instead gives world judges ten-year terms, renewable without limit [wcpa/1977_earth-constitution].

**Evidence.** Voeten found "some evidence" that career insecurity made Strasbourg judges favour their own governments [voeten/2008_impartiality-international-judges]. After terms became non-renewable in 2010, sitting judges became less likely to side with their nominating state, in preliminary results [stiansen/2019_institutional-reform-ecthr]. Across countries, only rules on selecting and removing judges are linked to real independence [melton-ginsburg/2014_de-jure-judicial-independence].

**For the Charter:** A single long term has good support. But the evidence says the candidate pool and the removal process matter more than term length. "Qualified candidates" has no owner in the draft. The Swiss vote shows public doubt about lot for judges.

## 4. Inspectors who answer to a court (IX.3)

**Investigating judges.** French prosecutors registered 4,299,473 new cases in 2024, and investigating judges opened 16,759 [france-ministry-of-justice/2025_chiffres-cles-justice]. Victims started 29.5% of these by civil-party complaint. Investigations closed in 2024 had lasted a median 29 months. The Outreau inquiry examined a case in which 13 accused were acquitted in 2004–2005 [assemblee-nationale/2006_outreau-inquiry-report]. It found that "la solitude du juge" (the judge's isolation) counted for a substantial part of the failures. It preferred collegial investigation to abolition.

**EPPO.** The European Public Prosecutor's Office takes no instructions from states or EU bodies [eu/2017_regulation-2017-1939]. Its chief serves one seven-year term, chosen from an expert panel's shortlist. But states nominate the delegated prosecutors who work in each country. At the end of 2025 it had 3,602 active investigations, with estimated damage of €67.27 billion [eppo/2026_annual-report-2025]. Authorities granted €1.13 billion in freezing orders in its cases in 2025. Governments can still obstruct. In 2021 Slovenia cancelled its two nominations, which the chief prosecutor called a "manifest lack of sincere cooperation" [eu-reporter/2021_slovenia-eppo]. Hungary joined only after its change of government, in July 2026 [eucrim/2026_hungary-joins-eppo].

**OLAF.** The EU's anti-fraud office investigates but cannot prosecute. In 2025 it closed 209 investigations and recommended €597 million for recovery [olaf/2026_olaf-report-2025-press-release]. Of its judicial recommendations from 2021–2025, national authorities had decided 73: 27 indictments and 46 dismissals [olaf/2026_olaf-report-2025-statistical-annex]. Another 72 awaited a decision.

**For the Charter:** Inspectors under the Court resemble investigating judges, who are slow and combine investigating with guarding rights. The EPPO model is stronger: an independent office brings its own cases, and courts approve coercive steps. OLAF shows that bodies which only recommend lose most cases downstream. Local nominations and cooperation remain weak points.

## 5. People answer, not populations (IX.5, IX.7)

**Individuals inside institutions.** The International Criminal Court tries only natural persons [icc/1998_rome-statute]. Official capacity "shall in no case exempt a person from criminal responsibility" (Art. 27). Superiors answer for subordinates they fail to control (Art. 28). The Earth Constitution applies world law to individuals acting for governments or corporations [wcpa/1977_earth-constitution]. The UK Supreme Court looks through a company only when someone uses it to evade an existing obligation [uksc/2013_prest-v-petrodel]. Statutes reach further. An officer who consents to, connives in or neglectfully allows a company's environmental offence is personally guilty [uk/1990_environmental-protection-act-s157].

Differs from the drafting-session research: its "ICC model" cannot reach companies, because the Court tries only natural persons [icc/1998_rome-statute]. IX.5 penalises institutions too.

**Ecocide.** The 2021 expert panel's definition requires "unlawful or wanton acts committed with knowledge" of likely severe damage [stop-ecocide-foundation/2021_ecocide-definition]. The damage must also be widespread or long-term. The EU's 2024 directive adds qualified offences that "can encompass conduct comparable to 'ecocide'" [eu/2024_directive-2024-1203]. These carry a maximum of at least eight years in prison. Company fines must be able to reach 5% of worldwide turnover or €40 million for most listed offences. Company liability does not bar prosecuting individuals (Art. 6(3)). Transposition was due by 21 May 2026; I did not check which states have done it. Belgium adopted a new Criminal Code with an ecocide crime on 22 February 2024 [stop-ecocide-international/2024_belgium-ecocide-timeline]. It carries 10–20 years and covers breaches of federal law or binding international instruments, or acts not located in Belgium. I could not verify when it takes effect. Vanuatu's Rome Statute amendment, co-sponsored by Fiji and Samoa [nz-herald/2024_pacific-ecocide-proposal], was circulated on 9 September 2024 [icc-asp/2024_wga-report-23-26]. In October 2025 Vanuatu said it would not table it formally that year [icc-asp/2025_wga-report-24-26]. I found no adoption as of October 2026.

**Collective penalties.** The Fourth Geneva Convention bans punishing anyone "for an offence he or she has not personally committed" [un/1949_geneva-convention-iv]. It also bans collective penalties. Broad sanctions hit populations, but the size of the harm is contested. Iraq's sanctions "undoubtedly greatly reduced the country's ability to import supplies of food and medicine" [dyson-cetorelli/2017_iraq-child-mortality-sanctions]. Yet the 1999 survey behind UNICEF's estimate of 500,000 extra child deaths was manipulated.

**For the Charter:** The draft's "knowingly" matches ecocide drafting. But pushing "the planet past the ceiling" is the sum of many acts, often lawful ones. Every legal model found here requires an unlawful or wanton act and severe harm. The Hungarian case also shows that withholding money from a government reaches its people.

## 6. Enforcement without armies (IX.6)

**The Earth Constitution.** Under Article 10, World Police "shall be armed only with weapons appropriate for the apprehension of the individuals responsible" [wcpa/1977_earth-constitution]. Searches and arrests need warrants from World Attorneys, who are prosecutors, not judges. The attorneys answer to the World Parliament, which can remove them by simple majority. Non-military means include denial of credit, revocation of corporate charters, fines and imprisonment.

**Routinely unarmed forces.** Police in England and Wales ran 17,584 firearms operations in the year to March 2026 [home-office/2026_police-use-of-firearms-2025-26]. They fired at people in 9 incidents, 0.1% of operations. The 6,558 armed officers were about 4.5% of 145,886 full-time-equivalent officers, though the counts differ in basis [home-office/2026_police-workforce-march-2026]. Police shot four people dead in 2025/26, against an average of three a year since 2015/16 [iopc/2026_deaths-following-police-contact-2025-26]. Deaths in or after custody were higher, at 27.

Norway has changed course. Since a June 2025 amendment, the Police Act lets the Police Directorate order officers armed in daily service [norway/1995_police-act]. The government proposed it after years of police requests, and four parties opposed it [nrk/2025_general-arming-proposal]. The Directorate's notice of general arming from 1 July 2025 could not be read [politiet/2025_general-arming-notice]. Ireland began a Taser pilot for 128 uniformed gardaí on 18 December 2025 [garda/2025_taser-pilot]. They carry them with body-worn cameras, and every use is notified to Fiosrú, the police ombudsman. New Zealand dropped armed response teams in June 2020, staying "a generally unarmed police service" [rnz/2020_armed-response-teams-ended]. Iceland's first death in an armed police operation came in December 2013 [bbc/2013_iceland-first-police-shooting].

**For the Charter:** Arrest-only policing is realistic, since unarmed patrols backed by small armed units are common. But the 2025–2026 trend runs toward more arming, and custody deaths outnumber shootings. The draft is stricter than its credited source, which lets prosecutors issue warrants. It does not say how officers act when a crime is in progress.

## 7. Restorative justice and humane confinement (IX.8–IX.9)

**Norway.** The Execution of Sentences Act requires that prisoners be offered a restorative process (§ 2) [norway/2001_execution-of-sentences-act]. It sets a duty of activity and, where possible, a gradual move from prison to freedom (§ 3). Halden's governor put the aim this way: "The life behind the walls should be as much like life outside the walls as possible" [guardian/2012_inside-halden]. A year at Halden cost about £116,000 per prisoner, against £45,000 in the UK (2012 figures). I could not open the correctional service's own statement of its normality principle.

**Recidivism.** The often-quoted claim is "just 20% after two years, compared with around 50% in England" [guardian/2012_inside-halden]. The 2026 Nordic statistics give two-year reconviction for prisoners released in 2022 [krus/2026_nordic-correctional-statistics-2020-2024]:

| Country | Released in 2022 | Reconvicted within two years | Rate |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Norway | 3,823 | 727 | 19% |
| Iceland | 162 | 22 | 14% |
| Denmark | 4,584 | 1,356 | 30% |
| Sweden | 8,712 | 2,743 | 31% |
| Finland | 2,282 | 924 | 40% |

The measure counts only new prison or community sentences, not fines, charges or rearrests. The editor warns that differences "still need to be interpreted with caution". A 2017 police reform may have affected Norway's later rates. The Guardian does not say how its English figure was measured.

**Causal evidence.** Bhuller, Dahl, Løken and Mogstad used the random assignment of cases to Norwegian judges [bhuller/2019_incarceration-recidivism-employment]. Prison cut the chance of reoffending within five years by 29 percentage points. The effect came from people without jobs before prison, who joined training programmes. Those previously employed suffered lasting job losses. I read the 2019 working paper; the 2020 journal version may differ slightly.

**Restorative justice.** Sherman and Strang found effects strongest for violent crime and crimes with personal victims [sherman-strang/2007_restorative-justice-evidence]. In a small sample of Aboriginal property offenders in Canberra, arrests rose. A 2013 review of 10 randomised trials found "a modest but highly cost-effective reduction in repeat offending" [strang/2013_campbell-rj-conferencing]. Victims were more satisfied, and both sides had consented. A police summary finds no significant effect for property crime, and the best results as a supplement to sanctions [college-of-policing/2026_rj-conferencing-toolkit]. A review of four trials with 1,447 young offenders found no significant effect on re-arrest [livingstone/2013_cochrane-rj-young-offenders].

**New Zealand.** Youth justice family group conferences bring the young person, family, victims and police together to agree a plan [oranga-tamariki/2026_youth-justice-fgc]. Victims' interests and the young person's accountability are among four primary considerations in law. I did not verify outcome data in this pass.

**For the Charter:** The evidence supports rehabilitative confinement, with voluntary restorative meetings added rather than substituted. The effects are real but modest. Norway's gradual release gives "a path home" a working legal form. Recidivism claims should rest on one defined measure.

## 8. Watching the watchers and open records (IX.10, IX.4)

**Police oversight.** Norway's Bureau for the Investigation of Police Affairs replaced a distrusted part-time system in 2005 [spesialenheten/2026_annual-report-2025]. In 2025 it received 1,305 reports and investigated 430. Only 34 ended in a fine, charge, indictment or waiver: 3% of reports. All its investigators have police backgrounds, a recurring criticism. For police shootings and cases of public interest, it publishes "the full decisions in anonymised form". England and Wales's IOPC received about 7,000 police referrals in 2024/25, mostly not investigated independently [iopc/2026_investigations]. It finished 72.5% of core independent investigations within 12 months in 2025/26, against a 75% target [iopc/2026_performance-framework-august-2026].

**Open courts versus privacy.** The European Convention lets courts exclude the public to protect juveniles or private life [coe/1950_echr]. The Rome Statute allows closed hearings to protect victims, especially of sexual violence [icc/1998_rome-statute]. EU law requires that authorities can stop the spread of information identifying a child victim [eu/2012_directive-2012-29]. England and Wales give sexual-offence complainants lifelong anonymity [uk/1992_sexual-offences-amendment-act-s1]. In 2021 the EU Court of Justice struck down Latvia's public register of traffic penalty points [cjeu/2021_latvian-penalty-points]. It held that disclosure "may give rise to social disapproval and result in stigmatisation". The 2024 environmental crime directive allows publishing convicts' data "only in duly justified exceptional cases" [eu/2024_directive-2024-1203]. UK law treats a spent conviction as if it never happened [uk/1974_rehabilitation-of-offenders-act-s4].

**For the Charter:** Existing rules protect more people than victims: suspects, the acquitted, children, witnesses and people with spent convictions. A permanent ledger of every action conflicts with EU data protection law and with rehabilitation. IX.4's "open hearing" lacks the exceptions every human rights court uses. Norway's Bureau shows a middle path: anonymised decisions plus full statistics.

## Tensions and open problems

- **Withholding versus no collective penalties.** IX.6 withholds "funds and benefits", while IX.5 bars penalties on communities. In Hungary the shield for recipients failed [ceps/2026_orban-defeat-conditionality].
- **Leverage worked through an election**, not through pressure on the sitting government [euronews/2026_hungary-unlocks-16bn].
- **Open standing overloads courts and favours the better-off**, in Colombia, India and Hungary alike. The draft has no filter.
- **No precedent for an allotted assembly choosing judges.** The nearest design lost 68% to 32% [switzerland/2022_federal-council-decree-vote-results]. Who certifies "qualified" is unstated.
- **Inspectors under the Court** may merge investigating and judging, the failure Outreau exposed [assemblee-nationale/2006_outreau-inquiry-report].
- **The ceiling is breached by many acts together.** No legal model found attributes that to individuals without an unlawful or wanton act.
- **Arrest only by court order** has no exception for crimes in progress, and "carry only what they need" has no test. Unarmed forces are arming more.
- **Open hearings (IX.4)** conflict with victim and child protection rules.
- **Open ledgers (IX.10)** conflict with data protection, rehabilitation and the presumption of innocence. The privacy exception covers victims only.
- **Restorative justice needs consent.** IX.8 does not say what happens when a victim or offender refuses.
- **The Ombudsman and the courts.** The draft does not say whether the Ombudsman may review judgments or only court administration.
- **Credits to correct.** The Earth Constitution lets prosecutors issue warrants and gives judges renewable terms [wcpa/1977_earth-constitution]. Nordic reconviction rates range from 14% to 40%, so "Nordic" is not one model [krus/2026_nordic-correctional-statistics-2020-2024].

## Options for the next draft

- **Shield and redirect.** When funds are withheld from a community's government, pay final recipients directly or route money to local bodies [eu/2020_regulation-2020-2092]. Set a date after which withheld sums lapse.
- **Voting rule.** Let a qualified majority trigger withholding, with no unanimity step like TEU Article 7(2) [eu/2016_teu-article-7].
- **Tiered standing.** Anyone may file in a community court; the Planetary Court selects cases for review, as Colombia's does [colombia/1991_constitution-art-86]. Add legal aid.
- **Judicial pool.** An expert panel with a lay chair certifies candidates [eu/2017_regulation-2017-1939] [uk/2005_constitutional-reform-act-sch-12]. An allotted assembly, or a draw, picks from the pool.
- **Judicial terms.** One 9–12-year term, an age limit, holdover until a successor sits, and staggered seats [germany/2024_grundgesetz-art-93]. Removal needs two-thirds of fellow judges after independent review [coe/1950_echr].
- **Inspectors.** An EPPO-style office that brings its own cases. A separate judge approves searches and arrests, and communities must cooperate.
- **Liability test.** Personal liability for knowing or reckless, unlawful or wanton acts causing severe harm [stop-ecocide-foundation/2021_ecocide-definition]. Add bans on directing organisations [eu/2024_directive-2024-1203].
- **Policing.** Allow arrests for crimes in progress, with prompt court review. Report every use of force to the Ombudsman, as Ireland does for Tasers [garda/2025_taser-pilot].
- **Hearings.** Add exceptions modelled on Article 6(1) of the European Convention and Article 68(2) of the Rome Statute.
- **Records.** Publish anonymised decisions and full statistics. Name only convicted people in serious cases, with expiry periods, and keep personal data off any permanent ledger.
- **Confinement.** Write in gradual release, a duty of activity and a restorative offer [norway/2001_execution-of-sentences-act]. Publish reconviction on one fixed definition.
- **Restorative justice.** Offer it to consenting parties, especially for violent crimes with personal victims, alongside other measures [strang/2013_campbell-rj-conferencing].
