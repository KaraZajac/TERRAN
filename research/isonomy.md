# Isonomy: equal law, the lot, and a name for Terran government

*Research note · planetary government, round 3 · first pass, 12 October 2026 · compiled by Claude, not yet reviewed by Kara*

Isonomia, "equal law", is first attested around 500 BCE, for health as a balance of the body's powers, then for government without a tyrant. Herodotus' Otanes gives it three marks: offices filled by lot, office held to account, and deliberation brought to the public. Athens did all three. It also elected its generals and some treasurers, checked every official before office and audited each one after, and counted only adult male citizens, by one estimate no more than about 16% of Attica's people. The word never named one constitution: oligarchs claimed it too, and scholars read it as a yardstick, not a form of government. Modern readings disagree: equality before the law (Hayek), no-rule (Arendt), equality secured by the freedom to leave (Karatani). The draft largely meets Otanes' marks and reverses Athens' exclusions. It falls short in three places: no audit at the end of office, an equal chance to serve rather than a turn, and limited exit from the Commonwealth after stage three.

## What the Charter says now

The third draft gives its form of government no name; neither "democracy" nor any other name appears in the Foundations or the Articles. The parts an isonomy would cover:

- **Lot decides (2.3, 3.3, 3.4).** "Terrans decide planetary questions through panels of terrans drawn by lot, chosen fresh for each decision." Assemblies of 450 draft; a fresh panel of at least 1,000 hears both sides and votes in secret. Enough terrans can call a vote of all terrans (3.5).
- **Public draws (4.3).** Draws use a public random seed, run on at least three independent operators and can be reproduced by anyone; no body oversees the draws for its own seats. Service is voluntary, paid and protected, and panel members are named only after their vote.
- **Certified pools (4.4, 4.5, 5.1, 7.2, 7.11).** Office-holders come from pools certified against published criteria. Two councils approve each Steward; officers and ombudsmen are drawn by lot from their pools; an allotted assembly chooses the judges.
- **Oversight and openness (5.2, 7.11, 2.5, 3.2).** An Oversight Council of 150, drawn by lot, approves, reviews and can remove Stewards. The Ombudsman council can investigate any institution, and its red team reviews each major proposal. Decisions and spending go on open logs, and open discussion maps common ground before assemblies draft.
- **No one rules alone (Article 3's opening, 2.8, 5.1).** "No single body holds enough power to rule alone." No body holds more than one power, and the Stewards hold no veto.
- **Who counts (Part I.1, 9.9, Schedule 2).** "Every person is a terran from birth." Until stage three only terrans who have joined are counted, and the voting age is not yet set.
- **Moving and leaving (Part IV.3, 2.9, 9.5, 9.8).** Any terran may leave any community and move to another. Members may leave the Commonwealth until stage three, and after it only if its safeguards fail.
- **A chosen chamber (2.10).** Member communities choose the Council of Communities' delegates "by their own methods", and the Council holds a second key (2.8).

## The word and its early uses

**The word.** Isonomia joins iso-, "equal", to -nomia. LSJ gives two senses: "equal distribution, equilibrium, balance", for Alcmaeon, and "equality of political rights", for Herodotus and Thucydides [lsj/1940_isonomia]. Nomos meant "usage, custom", and hence "law"; LSJ derives it from nemō, "distribute" [lsj/1940_nomos]. Some scholars take -nomia straight from that verb, which would make isonomia an equal share. Lane follows Vlastos in taking "law" as the root meaning, since it fits parallel words such as eunomia and anomia [lane/2024_greek-equality-under-law]. Vlastos' 1953 article could not be opened [vlastos/1953_isonomia].

**Alcmaeon.** Around 500 BCE the physician Alcmaeon of Croton is reported to have said that "the equality (isonomia) of the powers ... maintains health but that monarchy among them produces disease" (Diels-Kranz 24 B4, from Aëtius 5.30.1) [diels-kranz/1960_vorsokratiker-24b4; huffman/2025_sep-alcmaeon; lane/2024_greek-equality-under-law]. The report is a later summary. Most scholars, Huffman reports, trace its political terms to Alcmaeon; Mansfeld argues the summarisers added them; Huffman judges it "as probable as not" that Alcmaeon used them [huffman/2025_sep-alcmaeon].

**The drinking song.** Athenians sang that Harmodius and Aristogeiton "killed the tyrant and made Athens isonomous" (isonomous t' Athēnas epoiēsatēn) [athenaeus/1887_deipnosophistae-15-695]. The song gets its facts wrong. The man they killed, Hipparchus, was the tyrant's brother, and Hippias then ruled for four more years, "even more absolute than before" [herodotus/1920_histories-godley-5-55; thucydides/1942_historiae-1-20].

**Cleisthenes.** In the archonship of Isagoras, the fourth year after the tyrants fell, Cleisthenes made ten tribes "in order that more might take part in the government", and a Council of 500 [aristotle/1952_athenaion-politeia-ch21]. Lane dates this "inflection point" to 508/507 BCE [lane/2024_greek-equality-under-law]. He had won the people by "offering to hand over the government to the multitude", and the result was "much more democratic than that of Solon" [aristotle/1952_athenaion-politeia-ch20; aristotle/1952_athenaion-politeia-ch22]. Neither the Athenaion Politeia nor Herodotus' account of the reforms calls the new order an isonomia [herodotus/1920_histories-godley-5-66]. Herodotus credits Athens' rise to isēgoria, equal speech, which Godley translates as "equality" [herodotus/1920_histories-godley-5-78], and calls Cleisthenes the man who "gave the Athenians their tribes and their democracy" [herodotus/1920_histories-godley-6-131]. Only after Marathon were the nine archons drawn by lot, from 500 candidates chosen by the demes; since the tyranny they had all been elected [aristotle/1952_athenaion-politeia-ch22].

**Demokratia.** Herodotus uses the word without censure. He glosses Otanes' isonomia as the view that the Persians should "be governed democratically", and has Mardonius set up "democracies" in Ionia [herodotus/1920_histories-godley-6-43]. Thucydides' Thebans set an "isonomous oligarchy" and a democracy against the lawless rule of a narrow clique [thucydides/1942_historiae-3-62]. In civil strife, Thucydides writes, faction leaders were "each provided with the fairest professions": "political equality of the people" (isonomia) on one side, "a moderate aristocracy" on the other [thucydides/1942_historiae-3-82].

**What scholars make of it.** Huffman reports a common view that isonomia "is not a specific form of government but rather a concept of political equality in terms of which forms of government are evaluated": a "yardstick", or a "banner" [huffman/2025_sep-alcmaeon]. Scholars still dispute whether it began among aristocratic peers opposing a tyrant or with the democracy of the late sixth century [huffman/2025_sep-alcmaeon]. Lane puts its first attestations in the early fifth century [lane/2024_greek-equality-under-law]. Hayek thought the concept older than demokratia [hayek/2011_constitution-of-liberty].

**For the Charter:** The word suits a government of equals under law. But its record is as a banner claimed by several regimes, not as the name of one constitution, so the Charter would have to say what it means by it.

## Herodotus' constitutional debate

**The speeches (3.80–82).** Herodotus sets the debate among the Persian nobles who overthrew the Magi. He says the speeches "to some Greeks seem incredible, but there is no doubt that they were spoken" [herodotus/1920_histories-godley-3-80].

- **Otanes** wants no more monarchy. A sole ruler acts "with impunity" (aneuthynos, unaudited), and insolence and envy corrupt even the best of men. "The rule of the multitude" has "the loveliest name of all", isonomiē: "It determines offices by lot, and holds power accountable, and conducts all deliberating publicly." In the Greek, office is held hypeuthynos, subject to audit, and all deliberation is brought "to the common" [herodotus/1920_histories-godley-3-80].
- **Megabyzus** agrees about monarchy but not about the multitude. The tyrant acts "with knowledge, but for the other knowledge is impossible". So "let us choose a group of the best men... For we ourselves shall be among them" [herodotus/1920_histories-godley-3-80]. Rule by the knowledgeable is the subject of the companion note on [the qualified lot](qualified-lot.md).
- **Darius** argues that oligarchy breeds feuds and killing, and that popular rule breeds corrupt alliances until a champion of the people "is made their monarch". Both roads end in monarchy, so begin there [herodotus/1920_histories-godley-3-80].
- **The outcome.** Four of the seven back Darius. Otanes stands aside: "I desire neither to rule nor to be ruled". His house alone remains free, "so long as it does not transgress Persian law" [herodotus/1920_histories-godley-3-83].

**The vocabulary.** The Greek of 3.80–82 never uses the noun demokratia; Otanes speaks of "the multitude ruling" (plēthos archon) [herodotus/1920_histories-godley-3-80]. Roy notes that he avoids even dēmos, which may reflect "the 'bad name' that democracy might have", and that his marks leave out elections [roy/2012_constitutional-debate]. Herodotus later calls the proposal democracy [herodotus/1920_histories-godley-6-43].

**Authenticity.** Herodotus insists twice that the speeches were made [herodotus/1920_histories-godley-3-80; herodotus/1920_histories-godley-6-43]. The Persian setting and the abstract, sophistic form have led some scholars to argue that the debate came from another source, since constitutional debates "are viewed as quintessentially Greek events" [roy/2012_constitutional-debate]. Roy reports a "general consensus" that Otanes means democracy, yet "isonomia does not have to mean democracy" [roy/2012_constitutional-debate].

**Isonomia promised from above.** Maeandrius of Samos placed power "in the middle" and proclaimed isonomia, keeping six talents and a hereditary priesthood for himself. Told to "give an account of the monies", he lured citizens in one by one, as if to render it, and jailed them; his brother later had them killed [herodotus/1920_histories-godley-3-142]. Aristagoras of Miletus "made pretence of giving up his tyranny" and gave Miletus isonomia "so that the Milesians might readily join in his revolt" [herodotus/1920_histories-godley-5-37].

**For the Charter:** Otanes' three marks give a usable definition: lot, account, publicity. Maeandrius and Aristagoras show that a ruler can proclaim isonomia as a tactic, so the marks have to be institutions, not promises.

## How Athens used lot and election

**Lot for most offices.** In Aristotle's day "all the officials concerned with the regular administration are appointed by lot". The exceptions were a Treasurer of Military Funds, the Controllers of the Spectacle Fund and the Superintendent of Wells, elected by show of hands, as were all military officers [aristotle/1952_athenaion-politeia-ch43]. The ten generals, elected from all citizens, faced a vote in every presidency, and a general voted down was tried [aristotle/1952_athenaion-politeia-ch61]. Traffic ran both ways: the Clerk for the Presidency, once elected from "the most distinguished and trustworthy men", came to be drawn by lot [aristotle/1952_athenaion-politeia-ch54].

**Aristotle's theory.** "It is thought to be democratic for the offices to be assigned by lot, for them to be elected oligarchic" [aristotle/1944_politics-1294b]. His list of democratic institutions still includes "election of officials by all from all". It adds lot "either to all magistracies or to all that do not need experience and skill", no office twice except military ones, short terms, and pay [aristotle/1944_politics-1317b]. Liberty means "to govern and be governed in turn", and from it comes "the claim not to be governed, preferably not by anybody, or failing that, to govern and be governed in turns" [aristotle/1944_politics-1317b]. Socrates' accuser said he mocked "the folly of appointing public officials by lot, when none would choose a pilot or builder or flautist by lot" [xenophon/1923_memorabilia-1-2-9].

**The Council.** Five hundred members, fifty from each tribe, were drawn by lot [aristotle/1952_athenaion-politeia-ch43]. They had to be at least thirty [xenophon/1923_memorabilia-1-2-35] and could serve twice in a lifetime [aristotle/1952_athenaion-politeia-ch62]. The presiding chairman, also drawn by lot, held the keys and the state seal "for a day and a night" and could never serve again [aristotle/1952_athenaion-politeia-ch44]. An Agora handbook reckons almost every male citizen could expect a year on the Council [lang/1987_athenian-citizen].

**Jurors.** Any citizen over thirty who was not in debt to the treasury or disfranchised could sit [aristotle/1952_athenaion-politeia]. Fifth-century Athens kept 6,000 jurors [aristotle/1952_athenaion-politeia-ch24]. In the usual reconstruction, 6,000 citizens over thirty were chosen for a year and sworn by the jurors' oath, with a panel drawn for each case [bouricius/2013_multi-body-sortition; hansen/2016_authenticity-nomothesia-law]. The daily draw was among volunteers: those who wanted to sit brought their tickets at dawn [lang/1987_athenian-citizen]. Pericles introduced jury pay. Critics said the courts then declined, because "ordinary persons always took more care than the respectable to cast lots for the duty", and organised bribery of juries followed [aristotle/1952_athenaion-politeia-ch27]. By Aristotle's day a juror drew half a drachma a day and a councillor five obols [aristotle/1952_athenaion-politeia-ch62].

**The kleroterion.** Aristotle counts twenty allotment machines (klērōtēria), two per tribe [aristotle/1952_athenaion-politeia]. The man who slotted the tickets was himself drawn by lot, "in order that the same person may not always affix the tickets and cheat". Jurors then drew their court, so no one could "collect into a court whatever jurymen a person wishes" [aristotle/1952_athenaion-politeia-ch64]. Several such machines have been found in and around the Agora [papazarkadas/2011_athenian-kleroteria]. One, I 3967, has eleven rows of slots and holes for its lottery tube; it was found reused in a medieval floor near the Metroon [ascsa/2026_agora-object-i3967]. The surviving machines are late: the inscribed ones all date to the mid-second century BCE, and none can be confidently placed in the fourth [papazarkadas/2011_athenian-kleroteria]. The Agora handbook dates its illustrated example to the third century and thinks it served the Council, not the courts [lang/1987_athenian-citizen].

**Checks before and after office.** Every official, "both those elected by lot and those elected by show of hands", passed a dokimasia before taking office. The questions covered parents and deme, family shrines and tombs, treatment of parents, taxes and military service. Then came "Does anybody wish to bring a charge against this man?" [aristotle/1952_athenaion-politeia-ch55]. It tested standing and conduct, not skill. At the end, "all retiring officials" rendered account to ten auditors, and theft or bribery cost ten times the sum [aristotle/1952_athenaion-politeia-ch54]. When the demes began to sell the offices they allotted, Athens moved those draws to the whole tribe [aristotle/1952_athenaion-politeia-ch62].

**Lawmaking by lot? The nomothetai.** In the fourth century, new laws were passed by bodies called nomothetai. A law quoted in Demosthenes' Against Timocrates says they "shall consist of persons who have taken the judicial oath" [demosthenes/1939_against-timocrates-24-21]. Hansen defends that document as genuine and holds that the nomothetai were jurors [hansen/2016_authenticity-nomothesia-law]. Canevaro and Esu reply that this view "depends fully" on the document, which Canevaro judges a later forgery. They read Aeschines 3.38–40 as showing that the nomothetai were "a special lawmaking session of the Assembly" [canevaro-esu/2023_identity-nomothetai]. Canevaro calls his reading "the most economical interpretation of the evidence, not that it's safe" [canevaro/2019_nomothetai-response]. Bouricius's yearly 6,000 for "Courts and Legislative Panels" follows Hansen's side [bouricius/2013_multi-body-sortition].

**For the Charter:** Athens drew the many by lot and elected skill. It joined lot to two checks the draft only half has: a public hearing before office, open to anyone's objection, and an audit of everyone after it. Whether Athens ever made law by allotted panels is disputed, so the Charter should not lean on that precedent.

## Who counted

Only citizens governed. "In Athens all adult male citizens were admitted to the ecclesia" [hansen/1976_how-many-attended-ecclesia]. Citizenship came by descent. Pericles' law, passed "owing to the large number of the citizens", required two citizen parents. Young men were enrolled at eighteen after their deme voted on their age and free birth [aristotle/1952_athenaion-politeia-ch26; aristotle/1952_athenaion-politeia-ch42]. Women, enslaved people and resident foreigners (metics) were outside.

The numbers are estimates. Athenaeus reports a census under Demetrius of Phalerum that found 21,000 Athenians, 10,000 metics and 400,000 slaves [athenaeus/1887_deipnosophistae-6-272]. Hansen used the 21,000 as the citizen count of that time, against about 43,000 adult male citizens estimated for 431 BCE [hansen/1976_how-many-attended-ecclesia]. Akrigg has since argued that Attica held 400,000 people or more in 431. He counts 19,000–34,000 citizen hoplites plus 30,000–40,000 non-hoplite citizens, and at least 32,000 enslaved men in the mines and the fleet [simonton/2020_review-akrigg-population]. On his figures, adult male citizens were at most about 15–16% of the population (own calculation: 59,000–64,000 ÷ 400,000). Plato listed "equal rights in the relation of men to women" (isonomia, in his Greek), with slaves "no less free" than their owners, as the "climax" of democracy's excess [plato/1969_republic-563b].

**For the Charter:** Part I.1 makes standing a fact of birth, the reverse of Athens. The draft's remaining edges are the unset voting age and, until stage three, counting only those who have joined (9.9).

## Modern readings

**Hayek: equality before the law.** "Isonomia" came into English from Italian at the end of the sixteenth century, meaning "equality of laws to all manner of persons". Philemon Holland's Livy used "isonomy" for "a state of equal laws for all and responsibility of the magistrates". Then "'equality before the law,' 'government of law,' or 'rule of law' gradually displaced it" [hayek/2011_constitution-of-liberty]. The edition's editor quotes the Oxford English Dictionary's examples from 1659 and 1684; the second calls isonomy "the most agreeable and specious Title" of a popular state [hayek/2011_constitution-of-liberty]. Hayek reads the Greek ideal as the rule of law.

**Arendt: no-rule.** In On Revolution, freedom since Herodotus meant living "under conditions of no-rule, without a division between rulers and ruled". "This notion of no-rule was expressed by the word isonomy... The polis was supposed to be an isonomy, not a democracy." The word democracy, she writes, "was originally coined by those who were opposed to isonomy". Isonomy's equality was "an attribute of the polis and not of men, who received their equality by virtue of citizenship, not by virtue of birth", in a realm open "only to those who owned property and slaves". Her text is Otanes' "I want neither to rule nor to be ruled" [arendt/1963_on-revolution].

The reading has weak points. Otanes defines isonomia as the multitude ruling [herodotus/1920_histories-godley-3-80]. "Neither to rule nor to be ruled" is his reason for leaving the contest, not his constitution [herodotus/1920_histories-godley-3-83]. Herodotus uses demokratia without censure [herodotus/1920_histories-godley-6-131]. Aristotle treats no-rule as a democratic wish, and ruling in turn as its working form [aristotle/1944_politics-1317b].

**Karatani: equality through the freedom to leave.** Karatani's Isonomia and the Origins of Philosophy (2017; first published in Japan in 2012) contrasts "Athenian democracy with Ionian isonomia—a system based on non-rule and a lack of social divisions whereby equality is realized through the freedom to immigrate" [karatani/2017_isonomia-origins-philosophy; andersen/2023_review-karatani-isonomia]. In Andersen's summary, a landless Ionian "could instead migrate to the open frontier of the colony" rather than labour for a landowner or back a tyrant. So "the nomadic freedom of exit permitted the emergence of economic equality and its civic complement, the equality of isonomic non-rule". Andersen expects historians to find Karatani's picture of Ionia "excessively speculative", and asks what remains of exit once there is no frontier [andersen/2023_review-karatani-isonomia]. Only the book's front matter and introduction were read, not its chapter on Ionia [karatani/2017_isonomia-origins-philosophy].

**Lane: equal law, made.** Lane reads "equal law" three ways: equality under the law, in what the laws say; equality through law, in the rights and procedures that defend one's standing; and self-equalisation, the effort of making oneself an equal. "Equality of any kind is not just something that's found—it's something that's made" [lane/2024_greek-equality-under-law].

**For the Charter:** Each reading points at a different family of clauses: Hayek's at equal freedom and the courts (Part II, Article 7), Arendt's at the dispersal of power (Article 3, 2.8), Karatani's at the right to leave (Part IV.3, 2.9). A Terran isonomy would have to say which it means, or claim all three.

## Names for government by lot today

- **Demarchy, 1968.** Hayek coined it for limited government "in which the opinion but not the particular will of the people is the highest authority". His model legislature was elected, each generation voting once, "in their fortieth year" [hayek/1968_confusion-of-language].
- **Demarchy, 1985.** Burnheim took Hayek's "archaic word", noting that Hayek "did not employ it persistently". His demarchy abandons "elections, and in most cases referendums" for bodies "statistically representative of those affected by their decisions", chosen by lot. He calls the charge that such bodies "would not be accountable" "perhaps, the central objection to demarchy" [burnheim/1985_is-democracy-possible].
- **The Demarchists.** Alastair Reynolds' Revelation Space stories turn the word around: "all citizens had an implant which constantly interrogated them, eliciting their opinions on every aspect of Demarchy life", until participation "became near-involuntary" [reynolds/1997_spy-in-europa; wikipedia/2026_revelation-space-series].
- **Lottocracy.** Guerrero's 2014 article named it; it could not be opened [guerrero/2014_against-elections]. His 2024 book calls lottocracy "a new form of democracy", marked above all by "the shift from using elected representatives to using representatives selected through lottery" [guerrero/2024_lottocracy].
- **Open democracy.** Landemore builds on a large, randomly selected "open mini-public" and the ideal of "representing and being represented in turn" [landemore/2020_open-democracy].
- **Democracy, unqualified.** Van Reybrouck's Against Elections is subtitled "The Case for Democracy". Its publisher says that for most of democracy's history officials were chosen "through a combination of volunteering and lottery"; a blurb on the page calls it "sortitive democracy" [van-reybrouck/2016_against-elections].

**For the Charter:** "Demarchy" now carries three incompatible meanings: Hayek's limited, elected government, Burnheim's lot, and Reynolds' constant polling. "Lottocracy" and "open democracy" name particular designs. "Isonomy" belongs to no modern design, but its settled English sense is equality before the law, not lot.

**A chance, not a turn.** Athens could promise most citizens a year on the Council [lang/1987_athenian-citizen]. At planetary scale that is impossible: the round-two sketch put the odds of serving on a planetary body at about one terran in 1,950 over a lifetime ([sortition at scale](sortition-at-scale.md)). Terran isonomy offers an equal chance to serve, not a turn. Turns remain possible in communities, where most decisions sit (2.4).

**A frontier without land.** Karatani's Ionians owed their independence to open land. The draft's nearest substitutes are the floor (Part III) and the wealth limit (1.4), which aim to spare anyone from depending on a landlord or a strongman. That reading is this note's, not Karatani's.

**Darius' warning.** Darius predicted that popular rule ends with a champion of the people made monarch [herodotus/1920_histories-godley-3-80]. The draft answers with single-term Stewards who hold no veto (5.1) and emergencies that expire (6.1).

## Tensions and open problems

- **A banner, not a blueprint.** Isonomia named no single constitution. Oligarchs claimed it as readily as democrats, and rulers proclaimed it to win support [thucydides/1942_historiae-3-62; thucydides/1942_historiae-3-82; herodotus/1920_histories-godley-5-37].
- **Equal law or equal share.** The standard gloss is "equal law" or "equality of political rights" [lsj/1940_isonomia; lane/2024_greek-equality-under-law]. "Equal share" rests on the distribution reading of -nomia, which Lane, following Vlastos, sets aside.
- **The English word.** In English, "isonomy" meant equality of laws from the late sixteenth century until "rule of law" displaced it [hayek/2011_constitution-of-liberty]. A reader will not hear "lot" in it.
- **No-rule, or a turn?** Arendt's no-rule clashes with Otanes and Aristotle, and the draft keeps courts, enforcement and an executive. At planetary scale few terrans get even a turn.
- **Athens elected its experts.** Generals faced election and a vote in every presidency [aristotle/1952_athenaion-politeia-ch61]. Athens' checks tested standing and conduct, not skill [aristotle/1952_athenaion-politeia-ch55]; certifying skill has no model in the sources read here.
- **Lawmaking by lot.** Whether the nomothetai were sworn jurors or a relabelled Assembly is unresolved [hansen/2016_authenticity-nomothesia-law; canevaro-esu/2023_identity-nomothetai]. Schedule 2's pool of 6,000 jurors rests on firmer ground [aristotle/1952_athenaion-politeia-ch24]; a claim about allotted lawmakers would not.
- **Exit after stage three.** Karatani's isonomia depends on leaving, but once nations become communities within the Commonwealth, leaving it is possible only under 9.8.
- **Baggage.** Athenian self-government excluded most of the people, and Plato mocked isonomia between men and women [plato/1969_republic-563b]. The name invites the comparison.
- **Not verified.** Vlastos (1953) and Guerrero (2014) could not be opened. Karatani's chapter on Ionia, Akrigg's book and Hayek's Law, Legislation and Liberty were not read. The surviving kleroteria postdate Aristotle.

## Options for the next draft

- **The name.** Call the form of government an isonomy in the Foundations, with a one-line definition. Or use "Terran isonomy" only in the Articles and the Commentary, leave the form unnamed, or keep "democracy", as Van Reybrouck does.
- **The gloss.** "Equal law and an equal chance to serve" keeps both the Greek and the English senses. "Equal share" would need a Commentary note on the distribution reading.
- **Otanes' marks as the definition.** Lot for every body that decides (2.3, 3.4, 4.3), account for every office, and deliberation in public. Then name the exceptions: the certified pools, the Stewards and the Council of Communities.
- **An account at the end of office.** Every Steward, officer, ombudsman and judge gives a public account, with a window for anyone's complaint, as Athens required of all retiring officials [aristotle/1952_athenaion-politeia-ch54].
- **An objection before office.** Anyone may object, and be heard, before a certified candidate takes office, as in the dokimasia (4.4) [aristotle/1952_athenaion-politeia-ch55].
- **A regular confidence vote.** The Oversight Council votes on each Steward at set intervals, as Athens did on its generals, beside removal by two-thirds (5.1).
- **Public proceedings.** Assemblies deliberate in the open, with published records and stated exceptions (3.3).
- **Rotation limits.** Cap repeat service on allotted bodies, as Athens capped the Council at two terms [aristotle/1952_athenaion-politeia-ch62].
- **Who counts.** Fold the voting age and the stage-three rule (9.9) into the definition, so the name hides no exclusion.
- **Exit.** Settle the open question on leaving after stage three. The Commentary could present the floor and the wealth limit as the Charter's frontier.
