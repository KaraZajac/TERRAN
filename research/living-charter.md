# A living charter: amendment, interpretation and exit

*Research note · Charter Part XI, Principle VII, Part XII.5 and the open question "Leaving after stage three" · first pass, 10 October 2026 · compiled by Claude, not yet reviewed by Kara*

Part XI pairs an amendment rule harder than any precedent found here with absolute limits on amendment and a duty to read by spirit. Each choice has precedent. Together they move the last word from voters to whoever decides what would "lower the floor" and what the spirit requires: in practice, the Planetary Court. Evidence from second votes (Nevada) and popular two-thirds rules (New Hampshire, Nevis) suggests few amendments would pass, even simple repairs. Exit clauses elsewhere are rare and hedged. The best-supported option after stage three is exit from governance, but not from the floor, the ceiling or the Court's reach over past acts.

## What the Charter says

- Part XI opens: "This Charter can be wrong, so it is built to change." Part XI.1 ties revision to new knowledge (Principle V) and mistakes (Principle VI).
- Part XI.2: the Charter "is always read by its spirit". Principle VII goes further: where the wording conflicts with "compassion, wisdom, and justice, those prevail."
- Part XI.3: an amendment needs two-thirds of the votes in two planetary votes at least a year apart.
- Part XI.4: no amendment may lower the floor or weaken any protection under the Seven Principles; the ceiling moves only as the science does.
- Part XI.5: every 25 years an assembly drawn by lot reviews the whole Charter and proposes changes to all terrans.
- Part XII.5: any member may leave until stage three, and the Court keeps authority over past acts. After stage three the text is silent.
- Related: Part X.4 (second vote after a failed long-view test), Part VI.3 and Part VIII.10 (individual exit), Part I.1 (irrevocable standing), Part I.4 (not territorial).
- Part XI has no "draws on" note. The precedents below are echoed, not credited.

## Unamendable limits (Part XI.4)

Germany's Article 79(3) makes "inadmissible" any amendment affecting the Länder structure, their part in lawmaking, or the principles of Articles 1 and 20 [germany/1949_grundgesetz]. Article 1 makes human dignity inviolable; Article 20 sets out a democratic and social federal state. Other amendments need two-thirds of the Bundestag and of Bundesrat votes. Article 146 lets the Basic Law lapse when the people freely adopt a new constitution [germany/1949_grundgesetz].

Brazil's Article 60 §4 bars proposals "aimed at abolishing" federalism; direct, secret, universal and periodic suffrage; separation of powers; or individual rights [brazil/1988_constitution]. Other amendments need three-fifths of each chamber in two rounds. The core has not slowed change: the official text cites Constitutional Amendment No. 139 of 2026 [brazil/2026_constituicao-compilada]. The Supreme Court has invalidated an amendment under these limits (ADI 939, against Amendment 3 of 1993) [roznai/2014_unconstitutional-amendments-thesis].

Italy's Article 139 protects the republican form [italy/1947_constitution]. In decision 1146/1988 the Constitutional Court added implied "supreme principles that cannot be subverted or modified in their essential content" [roznai/2014_unconstitutional-amendments-thesis].

India has no written limit. In Kesavananda Bharati (24 April 1973), 13 judges held 7 to 6 that Parliament may amend any part but not the "basic structure" [pai/2023_kesavananda-golden-jubilee]. The Court gave no list; the doctrine grows case by case [roznai/2014_unconstitutional-amendments-thesis]. In 2015 it struck down, 4 to 1, an amendment creating a judicial appointments commission that included the Law Minister [vora/2025_why-sc-struck-down-njac].

Roznai coded 735 national constitutions. Unamendable provisions appear in 17% of those enacted 1789–1944 (52 of 306) and 27% in 1945–1988 (78 of 286). The share rose to 53% in 1989–2013 (76 of 143), and is 28% overall [roznai/2014_unconstitutional-amendments-thesis]. After 1945, nearly 30% of such provisions protected basic rights.

Practice is narrower than text. As of 2014, Germany's Constitutional Court had never voided an amendment under Article 79(3) [roznai/2014_unconstitutional-amendments-thesis]. Roznai finds "most of the world's unamendable provisions are non-self-entrenched". That invites a double amendment: repeal the limit, then the rule [roznai/2014_amending-unamendable-provisions]. Portugal did this in 1989, dropping its unamendable principle of collective ownership of the means of production. South Africa closes the gap: section 1 "and this subsection" need 75% of the National Assembly and six provinces [south-africa/1996_constitution]. Ethiopia's Article 105(1) also protects "this very Article" [ethiopia/1994_constitution].

**For the Charter:** Part XI.4 follows the post-1989 trend of protecting rights. It departs in three ways. It protects outcomes (the floor), which are harder to define than institutions. It does not protect itself, so Part XI.3 or Part XI.4 could be repealed first. And it has no valve like Article 146, or Switzerland's total revision, where voters can demand a rewrite and new elections follow [switzerland/1999_federal-constitution]. Whoever enforces Part XI.4 holds India's power, and India shows a court may use it in its own interest.

## Non-regression: "No amendment may lower the floor"

France's 2016 biodiversity law says environmental protection "ne peut faire l'objet que d'une amélioration constante, compte tenu des connaissances scientifiques et techniques du moment" [france/2016_decision-2016-737-dc]. In short: protection can only improve, in light of current science. On 4 August 2016 the Conseil constitutionnel held that this binds the regulatory power. The legislature stays free to amend or repeal earlier laws.

The UN Committee on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights is softer. Deliberately retrogressive measures "would require the most careful consideration and would need to be fully justified" (General Comment 3, para. 9) [cescr/1990_general-comment-3]. Retrogression is suspect, not forbidden.

The floor and the ceiling can collide. The team behind the 2018 "Good Life for All" study reports that no country meets basic needs at a globally sustainable level of resource use [leeds/2026_good-life-for-all-site].

**For the Charter:** Part XI.4 is stronger than both precedents. It binds the people's own amending power and allows no justification. Since the ceiling moves only with science, a tighter ceiling cannot be met by lowering the floor. If the two conflict, the Charter offers no lawful way out. France shows a ratchet can be tied to science without being absolute.

## Dead hand and Ulysses

Jefferson held it self-evident "that the earth belongs in usufruct to the living": the dead have "neither powers nor rights over it" [jefferson/1789_letter-to-madison]. Roznai treats unamendability as the sharpest dead-hand problem. He adds that blocking legal change can push change toward revolution [roznai/2014_unconstitutional-amendments-thesis]. Defenders reply that the people keep the power to write a new constitution.

Elster once cast constitutions as self-binding, like Ulysses at the mast. In *Ulysses Unbound* (2000) he revised that view, quoting the historian Jens Arup Seip: "In politics, people never try to bind themselves, only to bind others" [elster/2000_ulysses-unbound]. Elster adds that Ulysses "also put wax in the ears of the rowers".

**For the Charter:** The 2026 drafters would bind terrans not yet born, whom Part X treats as equals. The Part XI.5 review answers the dead-hand objection for most of the text, but not for the Part XI.4 core. With no exit after stage three and no route to a new constitution, Roznai's revolution risk applies in full.

## The amendment rule (Part XI.3)

| Polity | Rule | Popular vote |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sweden | Two identical Riksdag decisions with an election between; at least nine months from first submission to the election [sweden/1974_instrument-of-government] | Optional, held with the election; rejects only if no-votes win and exceed half the valid election votes |
| Sweden, pending | First decision by half the members, second by two-thirds; adopted 1 October 2025, awaiting the Riksdag elected in 2026; proposed start 1 April 2027 [riksdagen/2025_enhanced-protection-democracy; riksdagen/2026_amendments-pending-decision] | Unchanged |
| Netherlands | A law declares the change; after an election, both chambers adopt it by two-thirds of votes cast (Art. 137) [netherlands/1815_grondwet] | None |
| Denmark | Folketing passes; election; new Folketing passes it unamended (s.88) [denmark/1953_constitutional-act] | Majority of voters and at least 40% of all electors voting yes |
| Italy | Two debates at least three months apart (Art. 138) [italy/1947_constitution] | On request, unless two-thirds in both chambers |
| Switzerland | Assembly, or 100,000 signatures in 18 months [switzerland/1999_federal-constitution] | Mandatory; majority of voters and of cantons |
| Ireland | Bill passes both Houses (Art. 46) [ireland/1937_constitution] | Mandatory; majority of votes cast |
| United States | Two-thirds of both Houses, then three-quarters of states [us/1787_constitution] | None; 27 amendments, the last proposed in 1789 and ratified in 1992 [us/1992_amendments-11-27] |
| New Hampshire | Convention proposals need three-fifths of delegates [drapcho/2022_nh-ballot-measures] | Two-thirds of voters |
| Nevada (initiatives) | Citizen petition (Art. 19 §2(4)) [nevada/1864_constitution] | Majority at two successive general elections |
| St Kitts and Nevis (entrenched clauses) | Two-thirds of Representatives; 90-day wait (s.38) [st-kitts-nevis/1983_constitution] | Two-thirds on each island |

A correction to common summaries: Denmark's 40% is an approval threshold, not a turnout floor [denmark/1953_constitutional-act].

What the evidence says:

- Lutz (1994) found that length, difficulty and amendment rate interlock, across 50 US states and 32 countries [lutz/1994_theory-constitutional-amendment]. His index ranked the US most rigid [ginsburg-melton/2014_amendment-rule-matter].
- Ginsburg and Melton found that difficulty measures correlate poorly and "institutions are not the primary determinant of amendment rates" [ginsburg-melton/2014_amendment-rule-matter]. "Amendment culture", the previous constitution's amendment rate, predicts better.
- Second votes bite. In Nevada since 1980, 13 of 17 initiated amendments survived a second vote [fisher/2024_nevada-two-election-requirement]. The yes-share fell in 14, by 8.7 points on average. One fell from 72.4% to 33.0%.
- Two-thirds bites. In New Hampshire in 2022, removing an obsolete office won 63.7% on a partial count and failed [drapcho/2022_nh-ballot-measures]. Nevis's 1998 secession vote won 61.83% and failed [sudd/2018_nevis-1998-referendum].
- The Venice Commission advises against turnout and approval quorums. It accepts a qualified majority for "matters of fundamental constitutional significance" [venice-commission/2022_code-of-good-practice-referendums]. It also accepts multiple majorities for revisions in federal states.
- Definitions matter. Convention questions in Hawaii (1996) and Maryland (2010) drew more yes than no votes but failed against a majority of all election voters [dinan/2022_prevalence-state-conventions]. In Hawaii a court accepted that blank ballots count as no [dinan/2010_mandatory-convention-referendums].

Is Part XI.3 unusually hard? Yes. I found no polity that requires two popular votes, each at two-thirds. As rough arithmetic, not a forecast: with Nevada's average drop, a proposal would need about 75% the first time to keep two-thirds.

What follows? Ginsburg and Melton note that US judges "stepped in" because Article V is hard. Yet their data suggest judicial review may not substitute for amendment [ginsburg-melton/2014_amendment-rule-matter]. Under the Charter, pressure would flow to "spirit" readings and to the 25-year review.

**For the Charter:** Part XI.3 takes the Nordic delay and New Hampshire's threshold, then doubles both. It treats a clerical fix like a rights change, which sits badly with "built to change". It says "votes" without defining blanks or a base. It has no rule on support across communities, so a few high-turnout regions could carry or block a change.

## A review each generation (Part XI.5)

Jefferson wrote: "Every constitution then, and every law, naturally expires at the end of 19 years" [jefferson/1789_letter-to-madison]. He took 19 from Buffon's mortality table: half the adults alive at once die within 18 years 8 months. Repeal was no substitute: "a law of limited duration is much more manageable than one which needs a repeal." Madison replied on 4 February 1790. Periodic revision would make government "too mutable", "engender pernicious factions" and risk an interregnum [madison/1790_letter-to-jefferson].

Constitutions die young anyway: "only half live more than nineteen years" [elkins-ginsburg-melton/2009_endurance-national-constitutions]. Ginsburg and Melton argue flexible constitutions should last longer [ginsburg-melton/2014_amendment-rule-matter].

As of 2010, fourteen US states put a convention question to voters at fixed intervals [dinan/2010_mandatory-convention-referendums]. Eight did so every 20 years, Michigan every 16, four every 10, and Hawaii after nine. Voters had approved 30 such questions in US history, producing 25 conventions; half came from New Hampshire. Oklahoma had not held one since 1970 despite its rule. The last full convention met in Rhode Island in 1986; voters fear a "Pandora's box" [dinan/2022_prevalence-state-conventions]. New Hampshire gave a convention 33.9% in 2022 on a partial count [drapcho/2022_nh-ballot-measures]. Its last convention was in 1984 after a 1982 vote (Dinan) or in 1982 (NHPR); the sources conflict. Michigan requires the question in 1978 "and in each 16th year thereafter", so it is due in November 2026 [michigan/1963_constitution]. New York voted in 2017; I could not open certified results [nysboe/2017_convention-question-results].

Florida skips the "whether" vote. A 37-member revision commission forms in 2017 and every 20th year, and files proposals straight for the ballot [florida/1968_constitution]. The governor appoints 15, legislative leaders 18, the chief justice 3, plus the attorney general. Amendments need 60% of voters. Ireland's Convention on the Constitution (approved July 2012, sat 2013–14) had 66 randomly selected citizens, 33 politicians and a chair [citizens-assembly-ie/2026_convention-on-the-constitution]. Government accepted six proposals for constitutional change; two reached referendum in May 2015, and marriage equality passed with 62.1%. A blasphemy referendum followed in October 2018 [referendum-ie/2024_previous-referendums].

Constitutions also use sunsets. Canada lets legislatures override some Charter rights, but each override lapses after five years unless re-enacted [canada/1982_charter-of-rights].

**For the Charter:** Part XI.5 is closer to Florida than to Jefferson: a review, not an expiry. Lot follows Ireland, and sending proposals to all terrans avoids Ireland's government filter. Two gaps remain. The text does not say whether review proposals face two-thirds twice; if they do, Nevada and New Hampshire suggest little would pass. Nor does it say whether the review may touch the Part XI.4 core.

## Reading by spirit (Part XI.2 and Principle VII)

South African courts "must promote the values that underlie an open and democratic society" when reading the Bill of Rights [south-africa/1996_constitution]. When reading other laws they promote its "spirit, purport and objects" (s.39(2)). Canada's Supreme Court calls the "living tree" one of its most fundamental interpretive principles [canada-scc/2004_same-sex-marriage-reference]. The Persons case ([1930] AC 124) saw a tree "capable of growth and expansion within its natural limits" [canada-scc/2004_same-sex-marriage-reference; uk-jcpc/1929_edwards-v-canada]. In 2004 the Court rejected a "natural limits" objection to same-sex marriage: no agreed "objective core" defined those limits. Treaties start from the text: terms take their ordinary meaning "in the light of its object and purpose" [un/1969_vienna-convention-law-of-treaties].

Canada's 1998 Secession Reference used four underlying principles to derive a duty to negotiate [canada-scc/1998_secession-reference]. The same Court warned that such principles are not "an invitation to dispense with the written text". A written constitution, it said, "promotes legal certainty and predictability".

The risks are discretion and unpredictability. India's basic structure doctrine grew case by case and later protected judicial primacy in appointments [roznai/2014_unconstitutional-amendments-thesis; vora/2025_why-sc-struck-down-njac]. The usual checks are written reasons, precedent and override by amendment. Canada adds a five-year sunset on overrides [canada/1982_charter-of-rights].

**For the Charter:** The precedents read text in light of purpose. Principle VII lets compassion, wisdom and justice override the text. With Part XI.3 so hard, that makes the Court the main route of change. Judges chosen by an assembly and the Ombudsman do not answer who corrects a wrong "spirit" ruling.

Liechtenstein amended its constitution by a law of 16 March 2003 [liechtenstein/2003_constitutional-law-lgbl-2003-186]. The pages I opened do not confirm that this law added Article 4(2).

Duties can survive exit. A state leaving the ICC is not discharged "from the obligations arising from this Statute while it was a Party" [icc/1998_rome-statute]. Treaty duties created before termination survive (Vienna Convention Art. 70) [un/1969_vienna-convention-law-of-treaties]. The UN Human Rights Committee holds that ICCPR protection "devolves with territory", even through dismemberment, and allows no withdrawal [hrc/1997_general-comment-26].

Differs from the drafting-session research: it says the ICC "keeps jurisdiction over crimes committed while a country was a member". Article 127(2) is narrower: it keeps duties from membership, cooperation in investigations begun before withdrawal, and matters already before the Court [icc/1998_rome-statute]. Wider continuing jurisdiction rests on Court practice, which this pass did not check.

Differs from the drafting-session research: "the UK left in 2020" is right but compressed. The UK left on 31 January 2020, 34 months after notice, and stayed in the single market until 31 December 2020 [commons-library/2021_brexit-timeline].

**For the Charter:** Before stage three, Part XII.5 is an EU-style exit with an ICC-style tail, though its wording reaches further than Article 127. After stage three the Charter is silent. Every precedent either forbids unilateral exit (US, Canada) or hedges it with supermajorities, waits and published terms. Nevis shows the hedges bite.

## Theory: remedial or choice-based exit

Remedial-right-only theories allow secession only as a last-resort remedy. Buchanan names large-scale rights violations, unjust annexation and persistent breach of autonomy agreements [buchanan-levinson/2026_secession]. Plebiscitary (choice) theories, such as Wellman's, let a territorial majority leave if it can run a working state and the remainder still functions. The encyclopedia's authors argue remedial theories fit high-risk settings and plebiscitary ones low-risk settings.

Sunstein argued that "constitutions ought not to include a right to secede". He warned of blackmail, strategic threats and a de facto veto [sunstein/1991_constitutionalism-and-secession]. Buchanan's reply, in *Secession* (1991), is that supermajorities and waiting periods can contain those risks. He also argued a "bail out" option can persuade reluctant members to join a new union [buchanan-levinson/2026_secession; buchanan/1991_secession].

**For the Charter:** Part XII.5 already uses the bail-out logic before stage three, then ends it once joining is complete. Wellman's proviso maps onto the ceiling: an exit that leaves the remainder unable to keep the planet within limits would fail it.

## The planetary externality problem

Leaving a climate regime is real. The US left the Paris Agreement on 4 November 2020, rejoined in 2021, and left again on 27 January 2026 [untc/2026_paris-agreement-status]. Nordhaus found that without sanctions on non-participants, only minimal-abatement coalitions are stable; small trade penalties can hold a large one together [nordhaus/2015_climate-clubs]. Tools exist. The Montreal Protocol bans trade in controlled substances with non-parties [unep/1987_montreal-protocol]. The EU's carbon border charge (CBAM) entered its definitive regime on 1 January 2026 [european-commission/2026_cbam].

**For the Charter:** A community that leaves still shares the atmosphere. The ceiling can follow it through trade rules rather than force. After stage three the Commonwealth would be almost the whole world economy, so that pressure would be strong. Under "never by force" (Part XII.6), it needs limits too.

## Tensions and open problems

- **Power concentration.** Parts XI.2, XI.3 and XI.4 together make the Planetary Court the main amender. India's 7–6 origin and 2015 appointments ruling show the risk [pai/2023_kesavananda-golden-jubilee; vora/2025_why-sc-struck-down-njac].
- **No self-protection.** Part XI.4 does not protect itself or Part XI.3. Portugal shows "unamendable" clauses can fall in two steps [roznai/2014_amending-unamendable-provisions].
- **Floor ratchet versus ceiling.** The floor can only rise and the ceiling moves only with science. No country yet meets basic needs within a sustainable resource budget [leeds/2026_good-life-for-all-site]. The Charter has no rule for a conflict.
- **Unnamed science; vague core.** Part XI.4 does not say who certifies the science or how often. The phrase "weaken any terran's protection under the Seven Principles" is open-ended, and the Principles can conflict.
- **"Built to change" versus Part XI.3.** I found no precedent for two popular two-thirds votes. Clerical repairs face the same bar as rights changes. "Votes", blanks and any spread across communities are undefined.
- **Unclear interactions.** Does a failed long-view test (Part X.4) add a third vote? Which threshold do Part XI.5 proposals face, and may the review touch Part XI.4?
- **Spirit over text.** Principle VII is an override, not an aid to reading. Only a very hard amendment can correct a mistaken "spirit" ruling.
- **No exit, no valve.** After stage three there is no exit, no route to a new constitution, and an unamendable core. The drafting-session research flagged the no-exit risk; Roznai's revolution objection sharpens it.
- **What "leaving" means.** The Commonwealth is defined "not by territory or borders" (Part I.4), but the ceiling is physical. A leaving community keeps its land, air and emissions.
- **Part XII.5 wording.** "Authority over acts committed while it was a member" goes beyond Rome Statute Art. 127(2) [icc/1998_rome-statute]. Added on review: it matches ICC practice. In the Philippines case the Court kept jurisdiction over crimes committed while the state was a party, though the investigation was authorised after the exit took effect [icc/2026_philippines-situation].

## Options for the next draft

These are options, not decisions.

- **Tiered entrenchment.** Follow South Africa: the highest bar for Part XI and the floor, two-thirds twice for the Principles, a lower bar elsewhere [south-africa/1996_constitution].
- **A clerical-repair track.** An assembly by lot certifies purely technical fixes for a lower threshold, with Court review.
- **Define the vote.** Say "two-thirds of valid votes cast" and how blanks count. Avoid turnout quorums [venice-commission/2022_code-of-good-practice-referendums].
- **A distribution rule (optional).** Require majorities in a set share of regions, as Switzerland requires of cantons. This trades speed for breadth.
- **Self-protection.** Entrench Parts XI.3–XI.5 against amendment, as South Africa and Ethiopia do [ethiopia/1994_constitution].
- **Bound the floor ratchet.** Either adopt a "fully justified" test [cescr/1990_general-comment-3], or keep the bar and add a conflict rule. One example: the means of meeting the floor may change, but never its level.
- **Name the science process.** Say which body updates the ceiling, how often, and how the Court reviews it.
- **A constituent valve.** Let the 25-year assembly propose a total revision, as Germany and Switzerland allow [germany/1949_grundgesetz; switzerland/1999_federal-constitution].
- **Bound Principle VII.** Make spirit a guide to reading, not an override, as Canada does [canada-scc/1998_secession-reference]. Or publish any departure from the text and let it lapse after five years unless an amendment confirms it [canada/1982_charter-of-rights].
- **Clarify the review.** State the threshold for Part XI.5 proposals and whether the review may touch Part XI.4.

**Leaving after stage three.** These options can be combined.

- **A. No community exit.** Keep only individual exit (Part VI.3, Part VIII.10). Like the US, bar unilateral exit by sub-units, which Texas v. White allows only "through revolution or through consent of the States" [us-scotus/1869_texas-v-white]. Simplest for the ceiling, but it leaves the no-exit and revolution risks unanswered.
- **B. Exit from governance, not from duties.** A community may leave Commonwealth institutions. Floor rights stay with its people, as ICCPR rights do [hrc/1997_general-comment-26]. Ceiling duties continue through trade measures [unep/1987_montreal-protocol; european-commission/2026_cbam]. The Court keeps authority over past acts [icc/1998_rome-statute].
- **C. Hedged choice-based exit.** Two local two-thirds votes at least a year apart, mirroring Part XI.3. A clear-question test by the Court or an assembly by lot [canada/2000_clarity-act]. A successor constitution published six months ahead [st-kitts-nevis/1983_constitution]. A negotiated transition of up to two years [commons-library/2017_article-50-process]. Individuals may remain terrans and members (Part I.1).
- **D. Remedial exit only.** Exit only after a Court finding of grave, persistent Charter breach against the community [buchanan-levinson/2026_secession].
- **E. Mutual consent.** A local supermajority plus a planetary majority, echoing Canada's "two legitimate majorities" [canada-scc/1998_secession-reference].

B is the only option that directly answers the externality problem. It pairs with C, D or E.
