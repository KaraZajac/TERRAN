# Mixed panels: when ordinary people and experts sit in one body

*Research note · planetary government, round 3 · first pass, 12 October 2026 · compiled by Claude, not yet reviewed by Kara*

When lay members and professionals share a bench, the professionals usually lead. Lay judges in mixed criminal courts speak less, dissent rarely and seldom win. Swedish lay judges outvote the judge in 1–3% of cases, and in Córdoba eight lay members beat two united judges twice in 360 decisions. Yet random assignment shows lay members still change verdicts. Design shifts the balance: a majority required in each group, lay members voting first and by ballot, equal access to the file, facilitated small groups, experts who give evidence rather than conclusions, and lay control over which experts are heard. Bodies mixing citizens and politicians give uneven results. Ireland measured little domination at its facilitated tables, Britain and Brussels found more, and Ireland's politicians still pulled the one issue touching their own seats. No study tests a half-lay, half-expert body directly, but on this evidence a clear lay majority with these guards, or experts in a separate advisory role, looks safer than an even split. The weak point is that almost all the evidence concerns judging, not drafting.

## What the Charter says now

- **3.3.** Standing assemblies of 450 drawn by lot study proposals "with experts and Guardians" and draft planetary law. Different people recruit, brief and facilitate members, all rotated and audited.
- **3.4.** A fresh panel of at least 1,000 hears advocates chosen by the assembly's majority and minority, then votes in secret.
- **4.4.** Each certifying panel has "a majority drawn by lot from all terrans and experts drawn from the rosters". It publishes its reasons, and a refusal can be appealed.
- **4.5.** The independent offices are drawn by lot from certified candidates, and "the Earth Science Panel adds lay reviewers drawn by lot".
- **8.3–8.4.** Half the Guardians are drawn by lot from all terrans and half nominated by scientific bodies, for one ten-year term. Independent scientists prepare their impact statements.

Kara's working design would fill half of each drafting assembly and half the Guardians from a qualified lot, and keep the certifying panels' lay majority. Two companion notes cover the idea of isonomy ([isonomy](isonomy.md)) and lot among the qualified, from the IETF to research-funding lotteries ([qualified lot](qualified-lot.md)). This note asks what happens inside a body that mixes the two pools. Round 1 gave the Irish Convention's make-up ([deliberation](deliberation.md)); round 2 gave expert influence in France's climate convention and the Brussels committees' basics ([sortition at scale](sortition-at-scale.md)).

## Lay judges beside professional judges

Table sources: [germany/1975_gerichtsverfassungsgesetz-lay-judges; germany/1987_stpo-263-two-thirds; sweden/1942_rattegangsbalk; denmark/2025_retsplejeloven-891; japan/2004_saiban-in-act; france-ministry-of-justice/2026_jure-d-assises; assemblee-nationale/2025_cours-criminelles-departementales; bergoglio/2016_citizen-views-on-punishment; norway/1981_straffeprosessloven-332; kutnjak-ivkovic/2007_lay-participation-mixed-tribunals; anwar/2015_politics-in-the-courtroom].

**Germany.** Lay judges hold judicial office in full and with the same voting right as judges. Municipal councils propose them, a committee elects them for five years, and a public lot assigns them to about twelve sitting days a year [germany/1975_gerichtsverfassungsgesetz-lay-judges]. About 60,000 serve in criminal courts [machura/2016_understanding-german-mixed-tribunal]. Because a verdict against the accused needs two-thirds of the votes [germany/1987_stpo-263-two-thirds], two determined lay judges can block a conviction even against three judges [machura/2016_understanding-german-mixed-tribunal].

In practice the record is one-sided. Hans's review calls the consistent finding "minimal participation by lay judges and domination by professional judges" [hans/2008_jury-systems-around-the-world]. Casper and Zeisel found lay judges affected about 1% of verdicts; their article could not be opened, and the figure comes from Kutnjak Ivković [casper/1972_lay-judges-german-criminal-courts; kutnjak-ivkovic/2007_lay-participation-mixed-tribunals]. Rennig found that lay judges' chances "tend towards zero" unless they agree from the outset [kutnjak-ivkovic/2007_lay-participation-mixed-tribunals].

Machura explains why. A culture of consensus makes formal votes rare, so the rule that lay judges vote first seldom applies. Lay judges "need to be in a clear majority to have maximum influence"; in a minority, "lay participation is a mere formality". The presiding judge reads the file in advance and knows how past cases were sentenced. As one lay judge put it, "the judge can of course decisively control and influence the decision-making." A lay dissent prevailed more often when its holder had read the file, was given room to speak, and was younger. Still, over 70% of surveyed lay judges in Bochum and Frankfurt thought some cases would have gone differently without them [machura/2016_understanding-german-mixed-tribunal].

**Sweden.** One judge sits with three lay judges, nominated by parties and elected by councils for four years [sweden/1942_rattegangsbalk; anwar/2015_politics-in-the-courtroom]. In 2010, 46% of them were over 65 [anwar/2015_politics-in-the-courtroom]. Lay judges give their opinion after the legally trained members [sweden/1942_rattegangsbalk]. A survey found they outvote the judge in 1–3% of criminal cases [kutnjak-ivkovic/2007_lay-participation-mixed-tribunals; hans/2008_jury-systems-around-the-world].

Yet a natural experiment shows they matter. In Gothenburg in 2009–2012, lay judges were assigned by lot to 956 cases. A Sweden Democrat on the bench raised convictions of defendants with Arabic-sounding names by 17 points; a Left Party member raised convictions by 14 points when the victim was female. Recorded dissents on verdicts were rare (4%), but a simulation without peer pressure predicted about 20%: members switched losing votes to make decisions unanimous. Lay judges are "not inconsequential, even when deliberating in collaboration with a professional judge", and what they bring includes bias [anwar/2015_politics-in-the-courtroom].

**Japan.** Since 2009, six citizens drawn by lot have sat with three judges on serious crimes. A verdict needs a majority that includes at least one judge and one citizen, and judges rule on the law. The presiding judge must arrange "sufficient opportunities for the saiban-in to speak". Individual votes and their count are secret, so no one can measure outvoting [japan/2004_saiban-in-act].

By June 2026, 103,411 people had served. In 2025, 67.6% of those drawn were excused, and average deliberation had grown from 397 minutes in 2009 to 852 [supreme-court-japan/2026_saiban-in-trials-to-june-2026]. Of 4,513 who served in 2025, 79.7% found deliberation easy to speak in and 1.7% hard; 97.4% called it a good experience [supreme-court-japan/2026_saiban-in-survey-2025]. The courts run that survey, and their own ten-year review warns that lay ratings of the judges they sat with tend to be favourable (own translation) [supreme-court-japan/2019_saiban-in-ten-year-review].

Outcomes moved. The most common prison term grew heavier for several violent and sexual crimes, probation rose, and the range of sentences widened in both directions. In 2014 the Supreme Court told panels to start from the past sentencing trend as their common understanding, anchoring lay sentencing to professional precedent. Judges' study groups have asked whether they squeezed lay views into lawyers' existing frameworks. Appeal courts now defer more: in the main offences they reversed 10.9% of mixed-court judgments in 2012–18, against 17.6% of judges-only judgments in 2006–08 [supreme-court-japan/2019_saiban-in-ten-year-review].

**France.** A cour d'assises seats three magistrates and six jurors, nine on appeal, who vote by written ballot [france-ministry-of-justice/2026_jure-d-assises]. At first instance seven of nine votes are needed against the accused [assemblee-nationale/2025_cours-criminelles-departementales], so the judges cannot convict without four jurors (own arithmetic).

Since 1 January 2023, most adults accused of crimes punishable by fifteen or twenty years go instead to departmental criminal courts, where five magistrates decide by simple majority with the whole file before them. More than 85% of their cases are rapes. The stated aims were to stop downgrading rapes to lesser offences and to cut delays by simplifying scheduling and shortening hearings. A former justice minister said citizen assessors were ruled out because an earlier reform had shown "the limits of such a system". A 2013 evaluation of assessors in Dijon and Toulouse had found longer hearings. Appeals still go to a jury. By the end of 2024 the new courts' backlog had grown 57% in a year to 1,897 cases, which would take 17.9 months to clear, against 17.3 at the assizes. Acquittals ran at 4%, against 6%. A July 2025 parliamentary report rejected adding two citizen assessors and proposed benches of three career judges. France's Constitutional Council bars non-professional judges from forming a majority of a criminal bench, so at most two of the five may be honorary or temporary [assemblee-nationale/2025_cours-criminelles-departementales].

**Elsewhere.** Córdoba's lay majority shows that numbers alone are not enough. Of 360 decisions in 2005–2012, 79% were unanimous. When the two voting judges stood together against jurors only, the jurors lost 46 times and won twice. Where jurors differed from judges they were more lenient in 32 of 48 cases, and 84% of 715 former jurors said they could present their views. Yet a field study found that judges "observe, control and, if necessary, correct" jurors, who were shown the prosecution file against the law [bergoglio/2016_citizen-views-on-punishment].

In Korea, jurors deliberate alone but must hear the judge if they cannot agree. In shadow juries, every juror facing a "conclusion-inducing" judge changed position, against 23% facing judges who only gave information. The authors still judge that working together on sentencing benefits both sides [lee/2016_judge-jury-interaction-korea]. Denmark (from 2008) and Norway (from 2018) replaced independent juries with mixed benches, partly because juries gave no reasons [jackson/2016_lay-adjudication-in-europe; norway/1981_straffeprosessloven-332]. Norway lets the court make two of its five lay judges experts [norway/1981_straffeprosessloven-332].

**Across countries.** Judges agree with independent juries in 64–80% of cases, while mixed tribunals agree internally over 90% of the time. Hans suggests the gap "might be our current best estimate of deliberation influence". The presiding judge controls the file, the trial and lay questions, and in Germany writes the reasons even when outvoted. Influence can also run the other way: citizens can educate judges out of touch with community norms, and trained members can check prejudice [hans/2008_jury-systems-around-the-world]. Kutnjak Ivković traces dominance to legal training, a status tied to the task, which outweighs class or gender. One study found longer-serving lay judges less influential [kutnjak-ivkovic/2007_lay-participation-mixed-tribunals], but Machura's survey of administrative-court lay judges found years of service made no difference to winning a dissent [machura/2016_understanding-german-mixed-tribunal].

**Design implication:** Equal votes do not give equal weight. Lay members prevail only with a clear majority, the same information, a vote taken before the professionals speak, and a rule that an adverse outcome needs a majority of each group. Even then they rarely win outright; their value lies in the veto and in shifts at the margin.

## Citizens with politicians

**Ireland, 2012–14.** The Convention seated 66 citizens drawn by lot, 33 politicians and a chair [citizens-assembly-ie/2026_convention-on-the-constitution]. It deliberated at private tables of seven or eight, mixed and rotated, each with a trained facilitator and a note-taker. It decided by secret ballot, which stopped party whips [farrell/2019_mixed-membership-irish-convention].

Politicians were usually first to the microphone in public sessions. At the tables, note-takers counted 560 contributions by citizens and 356 by politicians, so a third of the members made 39% of the contributions. Asked whether some participants dominated, 14% of citizens and 9% of politicians agreed. Agreement peaked in the weeks on electoral and parliamentary reform, though the difference was not significant. Citizens mentioned "subtle ways" of steering, such as guiding the note-taker. On the electoral system, the one topic touching members' own seats, opinion moved from favouring reform to opposing it, the politicians' position [farrell/2019_mixed-membership-irish-convention]. Women spoke more than men in the facilitated small groups (abstract only) [harris/2021_womens-voices-irish-convention].

**Why Ireland dropped politicians.** The 2016 programme for government promised an assembly "without participation by politicians", and gave no reason [citizens-assembly-ie/2026_about-2016-2018-assembly]. Farrell and colleagues think the choice was "most likely due to the politically toxic nature of the abortion debate" [farrell/2019_mixed-membership-irish-convention]. Dublin's 2022 assembly mixed again, with 67 residents and 12 councillors [citizens-assembly-ie/2022_dublin-assembly-report].

**Britain, 2015.** Democracy Matters ran a citizens-only assembly beside a mixed one of 23 citizens and 6 councillors. After the first weekend, 31% in the mixed assembly saw domination, against 9% in the other. Four of the seven citizens who saw it, and seven of the ten who reported being influenced, named a politician. The tables were then rebalanced by party and by how active members were. By the second weekend the figures were 15% and 19%, but only three of the six politicians returned. The authors concluded: "At least in the short term, inclusion of politicians decreases the quality of deliberation" [flinders/2016_democracy-matters].

**Brussels, since 2019.** Committees join 45 citizens drawn by lot with 15 MPs [oecd-opsi/2022_brussels-deliberative-committees]. Because the Constitution lets only MPs sit in plenary, the citizens' vote is secret and consultative and the MPs' public and binding [simon/2023_dispatch-from-brussels; bertelsmann/2023_shortcut-9-deliberative-committees]. Fourteen interviews and observation found that the MPs' knowledge and debating experience let some of them exert "disproportionate influence", and that "the recommendations may be more the MPs' than citizens'". MPs also pressed the organisers outside the room to drop a topic; the organisers refused [simon/2023_dispatch-from-brussels]. In 2023 the francophone parliament recorded that the consultative vote frustrated some citizens, and put off making it binding [parlement-francophone-bruxellois/2023_report-article-42ter].

**The case for mixing** is uptake. Politician members may act as ambassadors for an assembly's conclusions, and the share of Britain's mixed assembly who found the politicians useful rose from 55% to 71% [flinders/2016_democracy-matters]. In Brussels the MPs who voted for the recommendations are the ones who follow them up [bertelsmann/2023_shortcut-9-deliberative-committees].

**Design implication:** Results split. Insiders were most prominent in plenary, on technical topics and on rules about themselves. At facilitated tables Ireland found little domination, and Britain found it fall once tables were rebalanced. Rotated tables, rebalancing and secret ballots may help; the evidence for each is observational.

## Experts inside citizens' deliberation

**France, 2019–20.** Round 2 recorded experts "unduly pushing for certain measures or discarding others". The same paper shows how. A technical group of 19 experts and a legal group of six worked between sessions: facilitators compiled proposals, technical advisers assessed them, lawyers reformulated them, and members resumed from the reworked text. Speakers' selection criteria were not explicit, advocacy was not told apart from expertise, and invited experts could not challenge each other's evidence. Facilitators sometimes stepped in so that citizens' views prevailed, "but this was not systematic", and a governance committee co-chair intervened as an expert. Members named external experts (92%) and the technical group (74%) as their main sources of opinion, yet some measures departed from what most experts suggested [giraudet/2022_co-construction-ccc].

The often-quoted "fifth" measures a division of labour. Members sat in one of five thematic groups and drew up only their own group's measures, then voted on all of them [giraudet/2022_co-construction-ccc]. It does not show that staff wrote the rest.

**Knowledge committees.** Ireland's 2016–18 assembly had an Expert Advisory Group that shaped the work programme and recommended speakers "for ratification by the Steering Group". That group was the chair and members elected by the assembly, and votes were by secret ballot [citizens-assembly-ie/2026_about-2016-2018-assembly]. The 2021 Global Assembly's nine-member knowledge committee shaped the framing question, the choice of experts and the learning materials [global-assembly/2022_report-2021].

**Witnesses.** In three Scottish citizens' juries on wind farms, witnesses doubted jurors' ability to weigh evidence. Jurors saw the "neutral" witness as slanted: "there's nobody to cross-examine him". The authors recommend a balanced oversight panel, jurors choosing witnesses, witnesses answering questions together, and published evidence [roberts/2020_experts-and-evidence-in-deliberation].

**When the expert states a conclusion.** In São Tomé, randomly assigned discussion leaders accounted in many cases for "over one-third of all variation in the outcomes" (abstract only) [humphreys/2006_leaders-in-democratic-deliberations]. In a binding classroom vote, moderators who voiced constrained views raised support for their option by 14 and 20.5 points [spada-vreeland/2013_who-moderates-the-moderators]. The Korean shadow juries point the same way [lee/2016_judge-jury-interaction-korea].

**Lay members as a minority.** Patients placed in English clinical-guideline groups "contributed infrequently", struggled with technical language, and saw their contributions "not subsequently acted on". A preparatory workshop helped but was resource intensive (abstract only) [van-wersch/2001_consumers-in-guideline-development].

**Design implication:** Who chooses the experts, who holds the pen between sessions, and whether experts give evidence or verdicts matter more than whether experts are in the room.

## Status and domination in small groups

The 1950s Chicago Jury Project found that upper-class men in higher-status jobs talked most and were most often chosen as foreperson; the 1957 original could not be opened [cornwell/2011_representation-through-participation; strodtbeck/1957_social-status-jury-deliberations]. Among 2,189 real jurors on 302 juries, participation still rose with education [cornwell/2011_representation-through-participation].

Rules change this. In 94 five-person groups deciding by secret ballot, women spoke less than 75% as much as men in most conditions. The gap vanished under unanimity when women were few, and under majority rule when they were many. Where women were the minority, a woman was voted most influential in 25% of unanimous groups and 13% of majority-rule groups [karpowitz/2012_gender-inequality-deliberative-participation]. The authors' book generalises the point: the right rule depends on the numbers [karpowitz/2014_the-silent-sex].

Structured, moderated settings weaken the jury-room pattern. Across five Deliberative Polls (1,474 people, 99 moderated groups), men and women talked equally. Groups moved toward the views of the more privileged about half the time, "a coin flip", and experts answered the groups' questions in separate plenaries [siu/2017_deliberation-and-inequality]. On an automated platform, 78% said their group "participated relatively equally" [stanford-ddl/2023_metaverse-community-forum]. Visible voting cuts the other way: in France a show of hands "threatens sincere voting" [giraudet/2022_co-construction-ccc], and Swedish lay judges switched losing votes to reach unanimity [anwar/2015_politics-in-the-courtroom].

**Design implication:** Decision rules, composition, moderation and secret voting change who speaks and who wins, but moderators can steer too. None of these studies mixes credentialed experts with lay members, so applying them to the qualified lot is inference.

## Keeping experts advising and people deciding

- **The Montreal Protocol.** At least a year before each assessment, held at least every four years, the parties convene panels of qualified experts, which report back to them [unep/1987_montreal-protocol-article-6]. The technology panel "does not evaluate policy issues and does not recommend policy". Its members serve "on a personal basis as experts", the parties appoint them, and its reports "must reflect any minority views appropriately" [unep/1996_teap-terms-of-reference]. IPCC reports "should be neutral with respect to policy" [ipcc/2013_principles-governing-ipcc-work]. How the Protocol's parties decide is in [polycentric governance](polycentric-governance.md).
- **Danish consensus conferences.** A lay panel of 14 sets the questions and helps choose the experts, and 12–15 experts answer. The panel writes its report alone; experts may correct facts "but they have no influence on the attitudes expressed by the panel" (a reproduction of the Board's own text) [danish-board-of-technology/2002_consensus-conference].
- **Magistrates in England and Wales.** The legal adviser "must not play any part in making findings of fact". Advice given in private is provisional and must be repeated in open court for the parties to answer [uk/2019_criminal-practice-directions-vi-trial].
- **Separate votes.** Brussels's citizens and MPs vote apart [simon/2023_dispatch-from-brussels]; Korea's judge speaks only when jurors cannot agree [lee/2016_judge-jury-interaction-korea].

**Design implication:** Separation works when the deciding body sets the questions, chooses or ratifies the experts, hears them disagree, and writes or votes on the text itself. It costs drafting craft: France needed a six-member legal group to turn proposals into law [giraudet/2022_co-construction-ccc].

## Design patterns that work

- **Double majorities.** Denmark needs four of six lay votes and two of three judges; Japan a majority with at least one of each [denmark/2025_retsplejeloven-891; japan/2004_saiban-in-act]. Each group can veto, and neither can rule alone. *Fails* if consensus norms mean no vote is taken [machura/2016_understanding-german-mixed-tribunal].
- **Lay members first, by ballot, with the split published.** Denmark's lay members vote first, and the verdict states each group's count and any minority's reasons [denmark/2025_retsplejeloven-891]. German law also has lay judges vote first [germany/1975_gerichtsverfassungsgesetz-lay-judges]. French courts and Irish assemblies vote by written or secret ballot [france-ministry-of-justice/2026_jure-d-assises; citizens-assembly-ie/2026_about-2016-2018-assembly]. *Fails* where a show of hands replaces ballots [giraudet/2022_co-construction-ccc].
- **A clear lay majority.** Needed for real influence [machura/2016_understanding-german-mixed-tribunal; hans/2008_jury-systems-around-the-world]. *Not enough alone*: Córdoba's lay majority beat united judges twice in 360 decisions [bergoglio/2016_citizen-views-on-punishment].
- **Equal information.** Lay judges who had read the file won dissents more often [machura/2016_understanding-german-mixed-tribunal]. *Costs* time and preparation [van-wersch/2001_consumers-in-guideline-development].
- **Experts give evidence, not conclusions, and face each other.** The ozone panels, the IPCC and Danish conferences [unep/1996_teap-terms-of-reference; ipcc/2013_principles-governing-ipcc-work; danish-board-of-technology/2002_consensus-conference]. *Fails* when one "neutral" expert goes unchallenged [roberts/2020_experts-and-evidence-in-deliberation; giraudet/2022_co-construction-ccc].
- **Lay control of the expert list.** Ireland's elected steering group ratified speakers; Danish panels help choose experts [citizens-assembly-ie/2026_about-2016-2018-assembly; danish-board-of-technology/2002_consensus-conference]. *Fails* where organisers invite experts at their discretion [giraudet/2022_co-construction-ccc].
- **Small facilitated groups, rotated and rebalanced** [farrell/2019_mixed-membership-irish-convention; flinders/2016_democracy-matters; siu/2017_deliberation-and-inequality]. *Fails* when facilitators take sides [spada-vreeland/2013_who-moderates-the-moderators; humphreys/2006_leaders-in-democratic-deliberations].
- **Insiders kept off rules about themselves.** Ireland's politicians moved the electoral-reform outcome toward their own interest [farrell/2019_mixed-membership-irish-convention].

## Tensions and open problems

- **Consensus against voice.** Unanimity norms hide disagreement [anwar/2015_politics-in-the-courtroom; machura/2016_understanding-german-mixed-tribunal], yet unanimity protects an outnumbered minority [karpowitz/2012_gender-inequality-deliberative-participation].
- **Secrecy against reasons.** Japan keeps vote counts secret; Denmark publishes them with reasons [japan/2004_saiban-in-act; denmark/2025_retsplejeloven-891]. Pressure for reasoned verdicts pushed Denmark and Norway from juries to mixed benches [jackson/2016_lay-adjudication-in-europe], but in mixed benches the professionals tend to write the reasons, in Germany even when outvoted [hans/2008_jury-systems-around-the-world].
- **Lay voice against bias.** Lay members import their politics as well as common sense [anwar/2015_politics-in-the-courtroom].
- **Lay voice against cost.** France removed juries from most rape trials to cut delays, and the backlog grew anyway [assemblee-nationale/2025_cours-criminelles-departementales].
- **Long terms.** Guardians serve ten years (8.3), and the evidence on whether long service erodes lay influence conflicts [kutnjak-ivkovic/2007_lay-participation-mixed-tribunals; machura/2016_understanding-german-mixed-tribunal].
- **Judging is not drafting.** Nearly all the evidence concerns verdicts and recommendations. Drafting law needs craft that most lay members lack, which is why France's lawyers held the pen [giraudet/2022_co-construction-ccc].
- **Insiders' interests.** Experts certifying their own field (4.4) resemble politicians judging electoral reform [farrell/2019_mixed-membership-irish-convention].
- **Weak measures.** Surveys run by the organisers may flatter, as Japan's own review says of its survey [supreme-court-japan/2019_saiban-in-ten-year-review].
- **Not found.** Any study of a body half lay and half expert; Japanese vote splits, which are secret by law; Norway's voting rule; the originals of Casper and Zeisel and of Strodtbeck [casper/1972_lay-judges-german-criminal-courts; strodtbeck/1957_social-status-jury-deliberations].

## Options for the next draft

- **3.3, three ways.**
  - *Separate bodies.* Keep each assembly wholly common lot. Add a qualified-lot drafting body that writes text on the assembly's instructions, publishes every change with its author, and cannot adopt anything.
  - *Lay majority.* Two-thirds common lot, one-third qualified lot. Adoption needs a majority of each pool, by secret ballot, with the common lot voting first and the counts by pool published.
  - *Half and half.* Keep the even split, but only with the same double majority and published counts, and with working groups chaired by common-lot members.
- **3.3, any option.** Let common-lot members choose or ratify the experts and witnesses. Require declared interests and at least two competing experts on contested points, and publish drafts between sessions. Keep facilitators independent, as 3.3 already does.
- **3.4.** Keep deciding panels wholly common lot; they are the clearest lay check.
- **4.4.** Keep the lay majority, and give lay members the whole file and time to read it. Vote by secret ballot, lay members first, with counts by group published. Then choose a double-majority rule: requiring both groups to certify means neither can admit a candidate alone; requiring both to refuse means neither can exclude one alone. Draw experts from fields next to the one being certified, not from it. Or make the experts non-voting advisers, on the English magistrates' model.
- **4.5.** Make the lay reviewers a panel large enough to speak as a body. It would set questions the Earth Science Panel must answer in writing, and its report would be published with the ranges.
- **8.3.** If half the Guardians come from each pool, count each half's votes separately on the finding that triggers 8.4's one-year wait. Requiring a majority of both halves lets either block the delay; letting a majority of either half trigger it keeps the brake out of the other half's hands. Or split the roles: the scientific half prepares statements, and the common-lot half decides findings. Consider shorter terms for the lay half, as [long view](long-view.md) suggested.
- **Schedules.** Record each mixed body's pool shares and decision rule as safeguards in Schedule 1, so a passing majority cannot tilt them.
