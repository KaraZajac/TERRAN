# The long view: future generations, guardians and precaution

*Research note · Charter Part X and Part III ("Guardians speak for the living world") · first pass, 10 October 2026 · compiled by Claude, not yet reviewed by Kara*

The Charter's "follow or explain" rule (X.5) is much broader than its Welsh model. That model is narrow and rarely used, and a Senedd committee said in March 2026 that it lacks the power to hold bodies to account. Guardians gained force from funding, independence and legal standing; Israel's was abolished after one term over cost and "too much authority". X.6's reversed burden has real precedents (Wingspread, Ecuador's Article 397, EU REACH), but "must show that it is safe" needs a workable standard of proof.

## What the Charter says

Part X plans "for thousands of generations and millions of years". X.1 makes unborn terrans' claim on the floor and ceiling "as strong as ours". X.2 bars risks "that could end the human story". Under X.3, Guardians speak for future terrans and the living world. Half are drawn by lot and half nominated by scientific bodies, for one ten-year term, with automatic successors. Under X.4, each planetary ballot carries the Guardians' impact statement, and a failing proposal waits a year for a second vote. X.5 says institutions follow Guardian recommendations or explain why not. X.6 gives Guardians court standing and puts the burden of proof on proposers. X.7 fixes their budget as a revenue share "that no vote or official can cut". Part III has Guardians speak "in every planetary decision".

## Wales: the Future Generations Commissioner

The Well-being of Future Generations (Wales) Act received Royal Assent on 29 April 2015 [wales/2015_future-generations-act]. Listed public bodies must set well-being objectives and take "all reasonable steps" to meet them (s.3). Section 4 sets seven goals: prosperous, resilient, healthier, more equal, cohesive communities, vibrant culture and Welsh language, and globally responsible. Section 5 uses the Brundtland definition of sustainable development. It requires bodies to weigh the long term, integration, involvement, collaboration and prevention [wales/2015_future-generations-act]. The duties covered 44 bodies in 2015 and 56 by April 2025 [audit-wales/2025_no-time-to-lose].

The Commissioner must "act as a guardian of the ability of future generations to meet their needs" (s.18). Welsh Ministers appoint after consulting a Senedd committee (s.17), for seven years (Sch. 2 para. 3). Ministers may dismiss for unfitness (para. 7), set the funding (para. 16) and approve staff numbers (para. 9(5)). A Deputy acts if the post is vacant (para. 11) [wales/2015_future-generations-act].

Section 20 lets the Commissioner review a body and recommend action. Under s.22(1), a body "must take all reasonable steps to follow the course of action set out in a recommendation". It may decline for "good reason" or adopt "an alternative course of action". Under s.22(4), "if the body does not follow a recommendation, the response must include the body's reasons" [wales/2015_future-generations-act]. This is X.5's model. The duty covers only s.20 review recommendations, not general advice or the periodic Future Generations Report (s.23).

The power is used sparingly. The 2022 review of the Welsh Government was the first Commissioner's second s.20 review [fgc-wales/2022_section-20-review-welsh-government]. The current Commissioner plans to "explore" using s.20 powers on renewable energy in 2026–27 [fgc-wales/2026_annual-report-2025-26]. I found no others, but did not search exhaustively.

Evidence on effect:

- The Auditor General (April 2025) found the Act "has increased prominence". He also found bodies giving "little or no explicit consideration to the Act". It is "not driving the system-wide change that was intended" [audit-wales/2025_no-time-to-lose].
- The Future Generations Report 2025 made 50 recommendations [fgc-wales/2026_public-body-responses], including a call to review the Act [fgc-wales/2025_future-generations-report-2025]. Of 55 bodies, 48 answered in full. In the 23 table rows I could parse, rejection never exceeded 4.3%. "Accept in principle" (agreement without firm plans) ran from about 4% to 46% [fgc-wales/2026_public-body-responses]. I could not open the 2020 report, whose site needs JavaScript.
- A Senedd committee report, *Fit for the Future?* (16 March 2026), found implementation "too slow". It said the Act "lacks the power to hold those public bodies to account". The chair asked the next government to "give it real teeth" [senedd/2026_fit-for-the-future-news]. I read the announcement, not the report.
- Haydn Davies (2016) judged that success "will depend heavily on the political will and the personalities" of those enforcing it [davies/2016_duties-or-aspirations].

On budget and independence, the office is "independent of but funded by the Welsh Government". It received £1,727,000 for 2025–26, spent £1.718 million, and had 23 full-time-equivalent staff on 31 March 2026. Its 2025–26 request would still have been below its 2022–23 funding [fgc-wales/2026_annual-report-2025-26]. In May 2026 the Commissioner proposed a Senedd Committee for the Future, citing Finland [fgc-wales/2026_senedd-for-future-generations].

Differs from the drafting-session research: it says public bodies "must follow advice or publicly explain why not". The legal duty covers only s.20 review recommendations and allows an alternative course [wales/2015_future-generations-act]. It also credits the Commissioner with stopping a motorway. The First Minister's 4 June 2019 M4 decision rested on cost and environmental impact. The Commissioner welcomed it as a "brave decision" [senedd-research/2019_m4-black-route-blocked]. Her influence is plausible but not shown.

**For the Charter:** X.5 widens s.22 to every institution and every recommendation. In Wales the duty yields published responses, not system change, and "accept in principle" is far more common than rejection. X.5 needs a definition of "following", response deadlines, and a named judge of explanations. The Charter already avoids Wales' other weakness: the Commissioner is appointed, funded and removable by the ministers under scrutiny.

## New Zealand: river and mountain guardians

The Te Awa Tupua Act 2017 makes the Whanganui River "an indivisible and living whole" (s.12) and a legal person (s.14). Te Pou Tupua exists "to be the human face of Te Awa Tupua and act in the name of Te Awa Tupua" (s.18). Its two members are nominated one by the river's iwi and one by the Minister for the Environment, and appointed jointly (s.20). Terms are three years and renewable. Since 30 November 2022 a member stays in office until reappointed or replaced (Sch. 3 cl. 1) [new-zealand/2017_te-awa-tupua-act]. The Act creates no "proprietary interest in water" and preserves existing consents (s.46). Te Pou Tupua runs a fund, Te Korotete (ss.57–59) [new-zealand/2017_te-awa-tupua-act], worth NZ$30 million [odonnell-talbot-jones/2018_legal-rights-for-rivers].

Te Urewera is a legal entity (s.11). Its board now has six members appointed by Tūhoe trustees and three by the Minister (s.21) [new-zealand/2014_te-urewera-act]. The Taranaki Maunga Act (assent 1 February 2025) makes the peaks a legal person (s.18). Its "human face and voice" has eight members, four appointed by iwi trustees and four by the Minister of Conservation (ss.28, 31) [new-zealand/2025_taranaki-maunga-act]. I found no evaluation of Te Urewera beyond the 2023 huts ruling in the drafting-session research. Taranaki is too new to judge.

O'Donnell and Talbot-Jones argue that river rights need three things: a suitable legal entity, independence from government, and funding. Whanganui had all three, but its role in water decisions was unclear. In India, a 2017 court named state officials as guardians of the Ganges and Yamuna, with no new funding. The Supreme Court halted that ruling on 7 July 2017 [odonnell-talbot-jones/2018_legal-rights-for-rivers]. Cribb and colleagues (2024) find little research on implementation. They argue the Act "should primarily be understood as recognising Indigenous rights and the authority of Indigenous law" [cribb/2024_beyond-legal-personhood].

Differs from the drafting-session research: it notes Te Pou Tupua nearly fell vacant in 2020. Parliament has since added a holdover rule (Sch. 3 cl. 1(3), in force 30 November 2022) [new-zealand/2017_te-awa-tupua-act].

**For the Charter:** these guardians are small, tied to one place, iwi–Crown co-appointed and short-termed. Their legitimacy comes from a Treaty settlement and an ancestral relationship. The Charter keeps the role but replaces that legitimacy with lot and scientific nomination. India shows the cost of guardians who depend on government and lack funding.

## Ecuador: rights of nature in court

Ecuador's 2008 Constitution gives nature "the right to integral respect for its existence" (Art. 71). Article 71 also lets "All persons, communities, peoples and nations" call on authorities to enforce those rights. Articles 72–74 add restoration, precaution against extinction, and a bar on appropriating environmental services [ecuador/2008_constitution]. Article 396 requires protection "although there is no scientific evidence of the damage". Article 397(1) puts "The burden of proof regarding the absence of potential or real danger" on "the operator of the activity or the defendant". Article 407 bans extraction in protected areas, except at the President's request with a legislative declaration of national interest [ecuador/2008_constitution]. On 16 November 2025, 61.8% of voters rejected a constituent assembly to replace this constitution [idea/2025_ecuador-november-2025].

On 30 March 2011 a Loja court upheld the Vilcabamba River's rights against a road project. It applied the "inversion of the burden of proof" and ordered a remediation plan within 30 days [greene/2011_vilcabamba-first-successful-case]. A year later, supporters called implementation "incomplete and careless" [garn/2012_vilcabamba-one-year-after]. Both sources are advocacy groups that backed the case.

In Los Cedros (10 November 2021), the Constitutional Court ruled seven votes to two. It held that these rights "do not constitute mere ideals or rhetorical statements, but rather legal mandates" (para. 35). Precaution applies where there is a risk of "serious and irreversible damage" and scientific uncertainty (para. 62). The Court confirmed two mining concessions' permits were void. It ordered the state miner ENAMI EP out of the forest, and asked the Ombudsman's Office to monitor a management plan (paras. 347–348) [ecuador-constitutional-court/2021_los-cedros].

Compliance by the state is weaker. Voters chose on 20 August 2023 to stop oil drilling in Yasuní's ITT block, and the Court set closure by 31 August 2024. Two years after the vote, only a handful of about 240 wells had closed. The block still produced about 44,000 barrels a day in the first half of 2025 [hrw/2025_yasuni-referendum-ignored]. I could not confirm the status after August 2025.

**For the Charter:** Ecuador is the closest precedent for X.6's reversed burden, which the Charter does not credit. Ecuador has no guardian office; it relies on open standing, as IX.1 does. Its Court limits precaution to serious and irreversible harm; X.6 says "serious or lasting". Courts can win cases while governments stall. Los Cedros' use of the Ombudsman fits the Charter's Ombudsman (VIII.9).

## Other institutions for future generations

- **Hungary.** Natural resources are a "common heritage" to preserve for future generations (Art. P). Deputy commissioners are "elected for six years with the votes of two-thirds" of MPs; one protects future generations (Art. 30) [hungary/2011_fundamental-law]. Before 2011, a separate ombudsman "could stop environmentally damaging legislation". The deputy now comments on draft laws and can prompt constitutional review [fdsd/2014_hungary-ombudsman].
- **Israel.** A 2001 law created a Knesset Commissioner for Future Generations. A panel of three Knesset members and three academics recommended candidates; the Speaker appointed. Terms were five years and renewable, with a separate Knesset budget line [knesset/2016_commission-for-future-generations]. The office lasted one term (2001–2006). Its delay power was "rarely invoked" because it drew antagonism. It was abolished over "the cost of its operations" and a feeling that it had "too much authority to interfere". One member objected to it "determining morally what is good and what is bad" [fdsd/2017_knesset-commission]. Differs from the drafting-session research: it dates repeal to 2010. My sources say only that the office ended in 2006.
- **Finland.** The Committee for the Future has been a standing parliamentary committee since 1993. Each electoral cycle it scrutinises the government's Report on the Future [fgc-wales/2026_senedd-for-future-generations]. A 2025 UN University survey counts at least 14 similar bodies. It lists Israel's and Mexico's as disbanded in 2006 [unu-cpr/2025_parliamentary-committees-future].
- **United Nations.** The Declaration on Future Generations (September 2024) asks states to promote "forward-looking, evidence-based impact assessments" (para. 26). It only takes note of a proposed Special Envoy, and plans a review at the 83rd General Assembly (para. 32) [un/2024_declaration-on-future-generations]. The envoy is "temporarily on hold" because of UN reforms and finances [un/2025_pact-paragraph-32a]. Differs from the drafting-session research: it says "never funded"; the UN says on hold.
- **Japan's Future Design.** In 2015–16, about 20 Yahaba residents met in four groups; two role-played residents of 2060. The final consensus kept "more than half of the measures originally proposed by the imaginary future-generation groups" [hara/2019_imaginary-future-generations]. In 2017 Yahaba sampled 1,000 residents at random and seated the first 26 recruited. Residents later proposed a water-rate rise, and the town collected 6% more the next year [saijo/2020_future-design]. These are small studies by the method's proponents.

**For the Charter:** the survivors sit inside parliaments or statutes, with modest powers and small budgets (Finland, Wales). Israel's office had the strongest blocking power and was abolished. Hungary's became a deputy post; my sources do not say why. The UN envoy stalled for lack of money. This bears on X.4's automatic delay and X.7's budget. Future Design offers modest support for the lottery half.

## Precaution and the burden of proof

| Text | Trigger | Burden | Limits |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Rio Principle 15 (1992) | "threats of serious or irreversible damage" | Not reversed | "cost-effective measures"; "according to their capabilities" [un/1992_rio-declaration] |
| Wingspread (January 1998) | "threats of harm" | "the proponent of an activity, rather than the public, should bear the burden of proof" | "open, informed and democratic" [sehn/1998_wingspread-statement] |
| TFEU Art. 191 | All EU environment policy | Not specified | Weigh "the potential benefits and costs of action or lack of action" [eu/2016_tfeu-article-191] |
| Ecuador Arts 396–397 | Doubt about impact | On the operator or defendant | Art. 407 exceptions [ecuador/2008_constitution] |
| REACH Art. 5 | Substances at 1 tonne a year or more | "No data, no market" | Tonnage thresholds [eu/2006_reach-regulation] |
| Charter X.6 | "serious or lasting harm" | Proposer "must show that it is safe" | None stated |

Cass Sunstein calls the precautionary principle "incoherent": risks lie on all sides, and precautions create their own. He prefers "a narrow Anti-Catastrophe Principle" plus cost-benefit analysis [sunstein/2005_laws-of-fear]. Sandin and colleagues reply that it is no vaguer than other rules. An evidence threshold or a de minimis rule can prevent absolutism [sandin/2002_five-charges]. Steel defends a coherent version [steel/2014_philosophy-precautionary-principle]. The European Environment Agency reviewed 88 claimed regulatory "false positives" and found four genuine ones [eea/2013_late-lessons-2]. The agency itself advocates precaution.

REACH is the largest reversed-burden regime. Its Article 1(3) puts safety on "manufacturers, importers and downstream users" [eu/2006_reach-regulation]. The Commission's 2018 review says "REACH shifted the burden of proof to industry". Before REACH, only 20% of existing substances had public data even for minimal screening. By December 2017, 65,000 dossiers covered about 17,000 substances. Yet "Compliance with the information requirements by registrants is considered insufficient" [european-commission/2018_reach-review]. I could not open ECHA's current data.

**For the Charter:** X.6 sits closer to Wingspread than to Rio. "Must show that it is safe" has no standard of proof, and absolute safety cannot be shown. Without weighing the risks of inaction, X.6 could block projects that cut larger harms: Sunstein's core objection. REACH shows a reversed burden yields data, but often poor data unless someone checks it.

## Long horizons and longtermism

Toby Ord's best guess is a "one-in-six chance of existential catastrophe over the next century" [ord/2020_the-precipice]. William MacAskill writes that the future "could last for millions more" years [macaskill/2022_what-we-owe-the-future]. Émile Torres argues longtermism makes non-existential disasters look minor, even climate harms that kill millions [torres/2021_against-longtermism]. Cremer and Kemp say the dominant approach "relies on a non-representative moral worldview" and should be democratised [cremer-kemp/2021_democratising-risk]. Nordhaus argued that the Stern Review's call for sharp, immediate action depends "decisively" on a near-zero discount rate and a specific utility function [nordhaus/2007_review-of-stern].

**For the Charter:** X.1 amounts to a zero discount rate on the floor and ceiling. Over "millions of years", sheer numbers would let future claims outweigh present ones. The floor (Part III) and the bar on lowering it (XI.4) stop trades that push present terrans below it. X.2 could still justify large sacrifices above the floor. The scale is also inconsistent: at 25 years a generation, a million years is 40,000 generations, not "thousands".

Table sources: [wales/2015_future-generations-act] [fgc-wales/2026_annual-report-2025-26] [senedd/2026_fit-for-the-future-news] [new-zealand/2017_te-awa-tupua-act] [odonnell-talbot-jones/2018_legal-rights-for-rivers] [knesset/2016_commission-for-future-generations] [fdsd/2017_knesset-commission] [hungary/2011_fundamental-law] [fdsd/2014_hungary-ombudsman] [coe/1950_echr] [germany/2024_grundgesetz-art-93] [germany/1951_bverfgg].

- **Protected budget.** Costa Rica's judiciary gets "no less than six percent of the ordinary revenues". Any amount above its needs is listed as surplus, with an investment plan, for the legislature to decide (Art. 177) [costa-rica/1949_constitution]. A World Bank review found earmarks can shield programmes "from shifting majorities". But "Governments also frequently withhold the allotted funds" or suspend the arrangements. Its verdict: "in general, it has not worked very well" [mccleary/1991_earmarking].
- **Single long terms.** Protocol 14 (adopted 2004) gave European human rights judges single nine-year terms "to reinforce their independence and impartiality". Replacements serve full terms, which staggers renewal [coe/2004_protocol-14-explanatory-report].
- **Automatic successors.** ECHR judges "shall hold office until replaced" (Art. 23(2)) [coe/1950_echr]. Germany's Basic Law has the same holdover. A federal law may also pass the election to the other chamber if one fails to act in time (Art. 93(2)–(3)) [germany/2024_grundgesetz-art-93]. After two months without an election, the Court is asked to propose candidates (§7a) [germany/1951_bverfgg]. Israel's deadlines did not stop the post lapsing after 2006 [fdsd/2017_knesset-commission].
- **Expert capture.** At FDA drug advisory meetings (2001–2004), 28% of voters disclosed conflicts and "only 1% of advisory committee members were recused". Excluding them would have narrowed margins but changed no outcome [lurie/2006_fda-advisory-conflicts]. One edited volume finds capture often "misdiagnosed" and "preventable and manageable" [carpenter-moss/2013_preventing-regulatory-capture]. The Israeli complaint about an outside body judging good and bad shows the technocracy risk [fdsd/2017_knesset-commission].

**For the Charter:** holdover rules and fallback selectors work where deadlines failed. Ten-year single terms match court practice. I found no lottery body that sits for ten years; Yahaba seated 26 of 1,000 people for three months [saijo/2020_future-design]. Fixed revenue shares exist, but earmarks are often bent.

## Tensions and open problems

- **X.5 outruns a weak model.** Wales limits the duty to formal reviews, and still "lacks the power" to hold bodies to account [senedd/2026_fit-for-the-future-news]. "Accept in principle" far outnumbers rejection [fgc-wales/2026_public-body-responses]. Nobody is named to judge explanations.
- **"No vote can cut" conflicts with Part XI.** An amendment under XI.3 could cut the budget, since XI.4 protects only the floor and the Seven Principles. Earmarks are often withheld [mccleary/1991_earmarking].
- **Who rules that a proposal "fails"?** If Guardians do, they hold a suspensive veto over every planetary vote. Israel's commissioner rarely used that power because it bred antagonism [fdsd/2017_knesset-commission]. Scope also varies: "every planetary decision" (III), "every major decision" and "each planetary ballot" (X.4).
- **X.6 has no standard.** Rio asks only for cost-effective measures [un/1992_rio-declaration]. The TFEU weighs the costs of inaction [eu/2016_tfeu-article-191]. Los Cedros requires serious, irreversible harm plus real uncertainty [ecuador-constitutional-court/2021_los-cedros].
- **Legitimacy without relationship.** NZ guardians rest on iwi authority and a Treaty settlement [cribb/2024_beyond-legal-personhood]. "Scientific bodies" is undefined, and without nations it is unclear which bodies nominate.
- **Two constituencies in one body.** Future terrans and the living world can conflict, for example over land for energy. The Charter has no rule for that.
- **Untested lottery terms.** Ten-year service will favour those able to serve that long. Unstaggered terms let a whole cohort leave at once.
- **Credits.** Ecuador has no guardians but supplies X.6's burden rule, which goes uncredited. The credited NZ guardians are nominated, not drawn by lot.
- **Scale and trade-offs.** "Millions of years" implies tens of thousands of generations, not "thousands". Read as an aggregate, "as strong as ours" invites longtermist trade-offs [torres/2021_against-longtermism].
- **Compliance.** Vilcabamba and Yasuní show orders and votes going unimplemented [garn/2012_vilcabamba-one-year-after] [hrw/2025_yasuni-referendum-ignored].

## Options for the next draft

- Limit X.5 to formal Guardian reviews, as in Wales. Add response deadlines, a public register, and Ombudsman review of explanations.
- Trigger the one-year delay only on a reasoned Guardian finding of likely serious, irreversible harm. Keep impact-statement scientists separate from advocating Guardians.
- Restate X.6: proposers show serious or irreversible harm is unlikely, with a de minimis threshold and the risks of inaction weighed. Credit Ecuador's Article 397 and Wingspread.
- Add the Guardians and their budget to XI.4. Consider a minimum share with a Costa Rica-style surplus return.
- Stagger terms, for example a fifth renewed every two years. Draw a ranked reserve list with each lottery and add a holdover clause.
- If scientific bodies fail to nominate in time, use a fallback selector, as Germany does; one option is lot from a qualified pool.
- Consider two-to-three-year lottery terms, with pay and support, beside ten-year nominated terms.
- Let communities tied to a place, including Indigenous peoples, name local guardians who work with the planetary Guardians.
- Read X.1 as an equal per-person claim, not an aggregate, and reconcile the two time scales.
- Consider separate seats for future terrans and for the living world.
- Add a Finland-style committee that answers a regular "Report on the Future".
