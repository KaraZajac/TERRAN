# A small, competent, accountable planetary executive

*Research note · planetary government, round 2 · first pass, 11 October 2026 · compiled by Claude, not yet reviewed by Kara*

The evidence favours a small collegial executive that a watching body chooses and can remove, not a planetary election. Competent administration tracks merit recruitment and protection from political purges more than pay or lifetime tenure. Digital services already run at national scale, but savings claims rest on assumptions, and the worst failures were unreviewable automated decisions. A thin planetary core of 30,000–60,000 staff would cost an estimated $4.5–15 billion a year, or $0.55–1.83 per terran.

## What the Charter says now

- **VIII.7.** A Council of Stewards, "elected by all terrans for one fixed term", administers without veto or army. Its size, term, decision rule and removal are unstated.
- **VIII.8–9, IX.3.** No body holds two of the four powers. An Ombudsman may investigate any institution; inspectors answer to the Court.
- **X.7.** The Guardians' budget is "a fixed share of planetary revenue".
- **VII.2, VII.5–6.** A digital identity from birth, open ledgers, and transparency that rises with power.
- **XII.3.** Planetary revenue, the floor and the wealth limit arrive at stage two.
- **Missing.** A civil service, an audit office, a statistics office, a list of departments and emergency powers.
- **Kara's steer, 11 October 2026.** Universal voting on every question is off the table, and she wants designs more modern than elections and representative assemblies. This note weighs digital-first administration, collegial and rotating leadership, independent agencies with open data, and AI with human accountability. Rules as code and experimentalist governance belong to another strand.

**Switzerland.** Seven members decide "as a collegial body", and each heads a department [switzerland/1999_federal-constitution]. The Federal Chancellery calls Switzerland "the only country in the world that has a collegial body as its government" [switzerland-fch/2026_brief-guide]. The 2:2:2:1 "magic formula" shared the seats among four parties from 1959 to 2003 [switzerland-fch/2026_brief-guide]. Members "cannot be removed" mid-term and usually serve until they retire [switzerland-fch/2026_brief-guide]. Parliament can still decline to re-elect them: Christoph Blocher won a seat in 2003 and lost it in 2007 [swissvotes/2019_vote-570-summary].

Voters have refused to elect the Council directly three times, in 1900, 1942 and 2013 [switzerland-fch/2026_brief-guide]. In 2013 the initiative won 23.66% and no canton [swissvotes/2013_vote-570-results]. The Federal Council warned that members would act as party politicians in a permanent campaign, against collegiality [swissvotes/2019_vote-570-summary]. It added that populous cantons would gain and Ticino would lose [swissvotes/2019_vote-570-summary].

The college can act fast. In March 2023 the authorities kept Credit Suisse solvent from Wednesday 15 March until the weekend takeover [parlament-ch/2024_pinc-credit-suisse]. After 79 hearings, a parliamentary inquiry found: "The emergency legislation was applied in accordance with the law" [parlament-ch/2024_pinc-credit-suisse]. It also found the Council "acted too hesitantly" on a liquidity backstop and was poorly informed in autumn 2022 [parlament-ch/2024_pinc-credit-suisse]. Emergency ordinances "must be limited in duration" (Art. 185) [switzerland/1999_federal-constitution]. The COVID-19 Act, the legal basis for pandemic ordinances, and two amendments all passed referendums [swissvotes/2021_vote-643-covid-act]. The votes were 60.20% (June 2021), 62.02% (November 2021) and 61.94% (June 2023) [swissvotes/2021_vote-643-covid-act] [swissvotes/2021_vote-650-covid-act] [swissvotes/2023_vote-664-covid-act].

**Uruguay.** José Batlle y Ordóñez proposed a Swiss-style colegiado in 1913 to "neutralize the dictatorial intentions of political leaders" [loc/1990_uruguay-constitution]. Its first version (1919–33) ended in a presidential coup [loc/1990_uruguay-constitution]. Under the 1952 constitution a nine-member council headed the executive: six seats for the winning party, three for the runner-up [loc/1990_uruguay-constitution]. It was "ineffective because the president lacked control over the ministers and because the majority was seldom united" [loc/1990_uruguay-constitution]. Inflation exceeded 50% a year in 1963–67, and factions in both main parties blamed the collegial executive [loc/1990_uruguay-blanco-administrations]. In November 1966 nearly 59% voted to restore a one-person presidency [loc/1990_uruguay-constitution]. That did not save democracy: the regime dissolved the General Assembly in June 1973 [loc/1990_uruguay-constitution]. Altman concludes that, even in this "most likely case", "institutions cannot simply be copied" [altman/2008_collegiate-executives].

**Bosnia and Herzegovina.** The Presidency seeks consensus, but two members can decide [bih/1995_constitution]. A dissenter can refer a decision as "destructive of a vital interest" of their entity; a two-thirds vote there blocks it [bih/1995_constitution]. The Venice Commission found: "A collective Presidency therefore does not appear functional or efficient" [venice-commission/2005_opinion-bih-constitution]. It wrote: "The main problem with veto powers is not their use but their preventive effect" [venice-commission/2005_opinion-bih-constitution]. It preferred one head of state, elected indirectly by parliament [venice-commission/2005_opinion-bih-constitution]. Seats reserved for three peoples also exclude others: a Jewish and a Roma citizen won the Sejdić and Finci case at Strasbourg [mrg/2025_sejdic-finci]. By July 2025, four electoral cycles later, nothing had changed [mrg/2025_sejdic-finci].

**The European Commission.** Members serve five years, must be "completely independent" and take no instructions [eu/2016_teu-article-17]. Parliament approves the college "as a body" and can force it to "resign as a body" [eu/2016_teu-article-17]. Censure needs two-thirds of members present and a majority of all members; none of 13 formal attempts succeeded [alemanno/2025_von-der-leyen-faces-the-vote]. Yet in 1999 the threat alone made the Santer Commission resign [eprs/2024_motion-of-censure]. Independent experts had found "a loss of control by the political authorities over the Administration" [european-parliament/1999_independent-experts-first-report]. They added: "It is becoming difficult to find anyone who has even the slightest sense of responsibility" [european-parliament/1999_independent-experts-first-report]. The crisis produced OLAF, the anti-fraud office [alemanno/2025_von-der-leyen-faces-the-vote]. The Commission employs about 33,000 permanent and contract staff [european-commission/2026_commission-staff].

**Against presidents and cabinets.** Across 14 indicators, Gerring and colleagues found "a strong relationship between parliamentarism and good governance" [gerring/2009_are-parliamentary-systems-better]. Cheibub accepts that presidential democracies die younger, but traces this to where they were adopted, chiefly military legacies (data 1946–2002) [toro/2007_review-cheibub]. Ganghof argues against "the constitutional concentration of executive power in a single human being" [tsebelis/2023_review-symposium-ganghof]. Government effectiveness scores for 2025 are 92.6 for Switzerland, 70.0 for Uruguay and 42.6 for Bosnia, out of 100 [worldbank/2026_wgi-government-effectiveness]. These are correlations: wealth and history differ as much as executive form.

**Lot and rotation.** In Athens "all the officials concerned with the regular administration are appointed by lot" [aristotle/1952_athenaion-politeia-ch43]. Military officers and a few finance and water posts were elected instead [aristotle/1952_athenaion-politeia-ch43]. The Council's presidency rotated among tribes every 35 or 36 days [aristotle/1952_athenaion-politeia-ch43]. I found no modern state that chooses its executive by lot.

**Design implication:** Collegiality works with a consensus norm, a majority fallback, a rotating chair and a selecting body that can unseat members. It fails with identity vetoes, a built-in opposition bloc, or responsibility so collective that nobody owns it.

## What predicts competent administration

Evans and Rauch scored 35 developing countries on whether core agencies "employ meritocratic recruitment and offer predictable, rewarding long-term careers" [evans-rauch/1999_bureaucracy-and-growth]. These features "significantly enhance prospects for economic growth" in 1970–1990, controlling for income and schooling [evans-rauch/1999_bureaucracy-and-growth]. In a companion study merit recruitment mattered most, while the effect of competitive salaries "could not be clearly established" [rauch-evans/2000_bureaucratic-structure-performance]. Across 52 countries, merit recruitment reduced corruption; salaries, career stability and internal promotion did not [dahlstrom/2012_merit-of-meritocratization].

The Quality of Government Expert Survey's 2020 wave covers 117 countries and 996 experts [qog/2021_expert-survey-2020]. My bivariate correlations from its country estimates, without income controls:

| QoG 2020 indicator | Correlation with impartiality | Countries |
|---|---|---|
| Merit-based recruitment | +0.81 | 79 |
| Patronage | −0.71 | 81 |
| Security of tenure | +0.71 | 78 |
| Closedness (entry only at the bottom, exams, special civil-service law) | −0.31 | 84 |

Merit also correlates −0.87 with political interference across 80 countries (own calculation) [qog/2021_expert-survey-2020].

Causal studies agree. British colonial governors appointed through patronage raised less revenue and invested less; the gap closed once patronage ended [xu/2018_costs-of-patronage]. In Brazil, a change of mayoral party replaced municipal school staff, and test scores fell 0.05–0.08 standard deviations [akhtari/2022_political-turnover-bureaucratic-turnover]. In Mexico, randomly higher salary offers drew abler applicants without lowering public-service motivation [dal-bo/2013_strengthening-state-capabilities].

Management matters too. Nigeria's federal civil service never started 38% of 4,721 projects worth about US$800 million [rasul-rogger/2018_management-of-bureaucrats]. A standard deviation more autonomy went with 18% higher completion; more incentives and monitoring went with 14% lower [rasul-rogger/2018_management-of-bureaucrats]. In Ghana, monitoring hurt least where tasks were clear [rasul-rogger-williams/2021_ghana-civil-service].

**Design implication:** The Charter should require open, merit-based recruitment and protect staff from political dismissal. Closed lifetime careers add little; autonomy for complex work and targets only for clear outputs add a lot.

## Independent agencies and their accountability

**Central banks.** In 16 OECD countries, politically controlled central banks were likelier to produce high, variable inflation, with little evidence of effects on growth or unemployment [alesina-summers/1993_cbi-macro-performance]. ECB board members serve one non-renewable eight-year term, removable only for incapacity or serious misconduct [ecb/2026_independence].

**Independence in ordinary law is fragile.** On 29 June 2026 the US Supreme Court overruled *Humphrey's Executor*, 6–3, making most independent regulators' heads removable at will [dla-piper/2026_slaughter-and-cook]. The same day, 5–4, it exempted the Federal Reserve, which "operates at a deliberate remove from the ordinary political process" [dla-piper/2026_slaughter-and-cook].

**Statistics.** The UN's Fundamental Principles, endorsed by the General Assembly on 29 January 2014, require methods chosen on "strictly professional considerations" [unsd/2014_fundamental-principles]. Agencies may comment on misuse and must keep individual data confidential [unsd/2014_fundamental-principles]. In October 2009 Greece revised its 2009 deficit forecast from 3.7% to 12.5% of GDP [eurostat/2010_greek-deficit-report]. A January 2010 Commission report found fiscal statistics "subject to political pressures and electoral cycles" [eurostat/2010_greek-deficit-report]. In 2013 the IMF censured Argentina over inaccurate price and GDP data [imf/2013_pr13-33-argentina-censure]. On 1 August 2025 the US president announced the firing of the labour-statistics chief, hours after a downward jobs revision [wikipedia/2026_bureau-of-labor-statistics]. The Senate confirmed a successor 51–47 on 7 August 2026 [bloomberg-law/2026_matsumoto-confirmed].

**Audit.** The Lima Declaration (1977) wants audit independence "laid down in the Constitution", with protected removal procedures [intosai/1977_lima-declaration]. Audit offices report "annually and independently", publish, and audited bodies must respond [intosai/1977_lima-declaration]. Publishing works. Releasing Brazil's random municipal audits significantly affected incumbents' election results, most where local radio spread the news [ferraz-finan/2008_exposing-corrupt-politicians]. Being audited cut later corruption by 8% and raised legal action by 20% [avis/2018_do-government-audits-reduce-corruption]. In Indonesia, raising audit odds from 4% to 100% cut missing road funds by 8 percentage points; grassroots monitoring did little [olken/2007_monitoring-corruption].

**Regulators.** The OECD's seven principles run from role clarity and undue influence to funding and performance evaluation [oecd/2014_governance-of-regulators]. Seventeen scholars argue that capture is often misdiagnosed and "may in fact be preventable and manageable" [carpenter-moss/2013_preventing-regulatory-capture].

**Design implication:** Independence held in ordinary law can vanish in one court term. Audit, statistics and money bodies need Charter protection, fixed budgets, single long terms, published outputs and removal only for cause.

The Royal Commission called Robodebt "a crude and cruel mechanism, neither fair nor legal" [robodebt-rc/2023_report]. Its launch package was expected to save $1.7 billion over five years [robodebt-rc/2023_report]. It recommended publishing business rules and algorithms "to enable independent expert scrutiny", and an audit body for automated decisions [robodebt-rc/2023_report].

The Dutch inquiry found "grondbeginselen van de rechtsstaat zijn geschonden": fundamental rule-of-law principles were violated [tweede-kamer/2020_ongekend-onrecht]. It blamed the tax administration, the legislature and the administrative courts, and noted the law had no hardship clause [tweede-kamer/2020_ongekend-onrecht]. In 2021 the data protection authority fined the tax administration €2.75 million [dutch-dpa/2021_tax-administration-fine]. Nationality had fed a system that "automatically designated certain applications as risky" [dutch-dpa/2021_tax-administration-fine]. The cabinet resigned on 15 January 2021 [wikipedia/2026_dutch-childcare-benefits-scandal]. By 30 June 2026 there were 69,679 registrations, 43,363 payments of at least €30,000 and €1.07 billion of public debts written off [netherlands-finance/2026_herstel-toeslagen-vgr23]. Late recovery decisions alone cost over €62.5 million in statutory penalties [netherlands-finance/2026_herstel-toeslagen-vgr23]. In 2020 a Hague court voided the law behind SyRI, a fraud risk-scoring system, as "insufficiently transparent and verifiable" [rechtbank-den-haag/2020_syri].

India's biometric checks in its largest welfare programme cut corruption, but 1.5–2 million legitimate beneficiaries lost benefits, mainly through how the transition was managed [muralidharan/2020_identity-verification-standards].

**AI in administration, as of October 2026.** The OECD reviewed 200 government AI uses: "many government initiatives remain in the pilot phase", partly for lack of impact measurement [oecd/2025_governing-with-ai]. In a 2024 UK trial with 20,000 civil servants, survey respondents reported saving 26 minutes a day [uk-gds/2025_copilot-cross-government]. That figure came from the midpoints of self-reported ranges [uk-gds/2025_copilot-cross-government]. A departmental evaluation "did not find evidence that time savings have led to improved productivity", and recorded hallucinations [uk-dbt/2025_copilot-evaluation]. EU law classes AI that decides access to essential public services as high-risk; those obligations now start on 2 December 2027 [european-commission/2026_ai-act-framework]. The UK makes departments publish algorithmic transparency records; GOV.UK listed 152 on 11 October 2026 [uk-gds/2025_atrs-hub].

**Design implication:** Federated, incremental digital services scale; big-bang launches and automated adverse decisions without a legal basis or human review fail badly. AI should draft and triage under published rules, not decide alone.

**Estimate: a thin planetary core.** Assumptions, labelled as such:

- Functions: a Stewards' secretariat and departments for the floor, ceiling, revenue, digital infrastructure and transition. Also the Court's registry, inspectors, Ombudsman, Guardians, audit and statistics.
- Staff: 30,000–60,000, from about the size of the UN's own staff to about double the Commission's.
- All-in cost per staff member: $150,000–250,000 a year. The only verified anchor is the Swiss Chancellery's CHF 257,000 per post (CHF 80 million ÷ 311, own calculation). I did not check international pay scales.

Arithmetic: 30,000 × $150,000 = $4.5 billion; 60,000 × $250,000 = $15.0 billion a year. Per terran: $4.5 billion ÷ 8.215 billion = $0.55; $15.0 billion ÷ 8.215 billion = $1.83. Against 2025 world GDP of $118.35 trillion, that is 0.004–0.013% [worldbank/2026_wdi-world-gdp]. The high case is under a quarter of UN system spending.

Excluded: transfers paid under the floor, the cost of votes and assemblies, and the digital backbone's running costs. I found no verified running-cost figures for X-Road, Aadhaar or Singpass.

**Design implication:** The core administration is cheap against these benchmarks; the floor's transfers and their delivery are what cost money. A Charter cap and a yearly published cost per terran would keep the core small.

## Design patterns that work

- **A small college with a rotating chair.** Five to nine members, consensus first, majority fallback, no veto, chair rotating yearly (Switzerland, Earth Constitution). It needs a selecting body that can unseat members, and rules forcing information-sharing. It fails with identity vetoes (Bosnia), a built-in opposition bloc (Uruguay) or ministers who keep colleagues uninformed (Credit Suisse).
- **Selection by a watching body.** Parliaments choose the Swiss Council and confirm the Commission, and can unseat or censure them. An allotted chamber choosing from a certified pool is the modern variant, untested for executives. It fails when removal is so hard that only threats work.
- **A merit civil service shielded from politics.** Open competition, protection from purges, competitive pay and autonomy for complex work. It fails through patronage, post-election purges and blanket targets.
- **Entrenched independent agencies, open by default.** Audit, statistics and money bodies with single long terms and published outputs; published audits change behaviour when media carry them. Open data alone is not enough: in Indonesia, citizen monitoring did little next to professional audits. They fail when independence rests on ordinary law or ministers control the numbers.
- **Federated digital services, built in increments.** Agencies keep their own data and exchange it over one secure layer; citizens get one login and an offline route. They fail through big-bang launches, neglected maintenance and identity checks that exclude people.
- **AI with human accountability.** A legal basis first, a public algorithm register, human review and appeal, and independent audit. The evidence is mostly negative lessons; productivity gains are self-reported so far.

## Tensions and open problems

- **A planetary election (VIII.7).** None has ever covered humanity, Swiss voters keep rejecting even national direct election, and Kara's steer moves away from mass votes.
- **One term, no removal (VIII.7).** A single term removes the re-election lever, and nothing replaces it.
- **Who selects?** The Charter has no standing legislature, so a selecting body must be designed: allotted, delegated or elected.
- **Balance without quotas.** Bosnia's reserved seats excluded citizens; Swiss "appropriate representation" is a norm, not a quota.
- **Collective versus individual responsibility.** Collegiality spreads blame, as the Santer report showed.
- **Agencies and VIII.8.** Central banks and regulators make rules, operate and sanction, a mix VIII.8 forbids in one body.
- **Digital-first versus access.** Online identity checks and biometrics shut out eligible people in the UK and India; VII.2 carries the same risk (see [digital voting](digital-voting.md)).
- **Speed versus legality.** Robodebt and the Dutch scheme scaled errors for years before courts and inquiries caught them.
- **Emergencies without referendums.** Swiss voters ratified COVID powers three times; without universal votes, another body must ratify.
- **Unknown costs.** No verified running costs exist for national digital backbones.

## Options for the next draft

- **VIII.7, selection.** Replace direct election by all terrans. Option A: a standing allotted chamber elects Stewards from a pool certified by an independent panel (see [sortition at scale](sortition-at-scale.md)). Option B: delegates of member communities elect them, the best-evidenced route. Option C: one planetary multi-seat vote, the weakest on the evidence.
- **VIII.7, form.** Seven to nine Stewards, no two from one world region, a chair rotating yearly, consensus first and majority after, no veto.
- **VIII.7, term and removal.** One non-renewable term of about six years, staggered. Removal individually or together by a qualified majority of the selecting body, and by the Court for misconduct.
- **New clause, emergencies.** Time-limited emergency orders that lapse unless the selecting body confirms them, followed by an inquiry with hearing powers.
- **New clause, civil service.** Open merit competition, no political appointments below the Stewards, dismissal only for cause, and independently set pay.
- **New clause, independent agencies.** Audit, statistics, money and identity-and-voting bodies outside the Stewards. Single long terms, fixed budget shares like X.7, and open data by default.
- **New clause, automated decisions.** No adverse decision by algorithm alone: a legal basis, a public register, human review, appeal and audit.
- **VII.2 and VII.5.** Federated data exchange, open-source code, a record of who used each terran's data, and an offline route to every service.
- **XII.3, cost.** Cap core administration, for example at 0.02% of members' combined GDP, and publish the cost per terran each year.

## Design sketch: the Council of Stewards and the planetary civil service

One option assembled from the evidence above, not a decision.

**The Council of Stewards**

- **Size and portfolios.** Seven Stewards, each heading a department: the floor; the ceiling; revenue and the commons; digital infrastructure; health and resilience; transition; the civil service.
- **Selection.** An independent panel certifies a public pool against published criteria. A standing allotted chamber questions candidates in public and elects the college as a slate, by two-thirds. There is no planetary campaign.
- **Balance and chair.** No two Stewards from one world region, and no seats by faith, ethnicity or ancestry. The chair rotates yearly in a fixed order, with no casting vote.
- **Term and removal.** One six-year term, with two or three seats changing every two years. Two-thirds of the selecting chamber can remove one Steward or all.
- **Decisions.** Consensus first, then majority; no veto. Each Steward answers personally for a department, and minutes are published after a fixed delay.
- **Emergencies.** Orders of limited duration lapse unless the chamber confirms them; an inquiry follows.
- **Support.** A secretariat of about 300, the size of the Swiss Chancellery.

**The planetary civil service**

- **Size.** 30,000–60,000 staff, growing only as stage two adds functions.
- **People.** Open competitive exams, online and in person, with regional spread targets. Department heads come from a senior civil-service list, as in the Earth Constitution [wcpa/1977_earth-constitution]. Staff are protected from political dismissal, and pay is set independently.
- **Management.** Autonomy for complex work; targets only for measurable outputs.
- **Independent bodies.** Audit Office, Statistics Office, money board, Identity and Voting Authority, Ombudsman, inspectors and Guardians, each with a fixed budget and open data.
- **Digital backbone.** Federated exchange on the X-Road pattern, open-source code, an offline route to every service, and small releases with named owners.
- **AI.** Drafting, search and triage under a public algorithm register; human review and appeal for any adverse decision; independent audit of high-risk systems.
- **Cost.** An estimated $4.5–15 billion a year ($0.55–1.83 per terran), published annually, excluding transfers, votes and the digital backbone.
