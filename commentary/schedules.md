# Commentary on the Schedules

*Not binding. Why the Schedules hold the numbers they do, with the evidence behind them · drafted by Claude from the research notes, 12 October 2026 · where this reading and Kara's intent differ, hers governs*

The research notes and thoughts cite the second working draft's numbering; its one schedule, of world regions, is now Schedule 3, and the [changelog](../CHANGELOG.md#third-working-draft--12-october-2026) maps the rest.

## Why the numbers live in schedules

The Schedules hold the numbers and lists the Articles use, each with its reason and, where the research gives one, a range, so each can be checked and changed in one place. The Foundations keep their few numbers in their own text.

**Why it says this:** One long document mixed three kinds of text that should change at different speeds: lasting principles, the machinery of government, and numbers that need tuning ([changelog](../CHANGELOG.md#third-working-draft--12-october-2026)). Many numbers are still defaults: the government design called its sizes and terms "starting points from the research, for Kara to set" ([the government design](../proposals/modern-terran-government.md)). Numbers need tuning in use: Ostbelgien's decree allowed up to three assemblies a year, an evaluation judged that too heavy, and a 2024 amendment capped them at five per parliamentary term ([sortition at scale](../research/sortition-at-scale.md)). One wrong number can defeat an institution: Estonia's rule that over half the roll must vote closed the Finnish minority's cultural autonomy in 2023 ([polycentric governance](../research/polycentric-governance.md)). Every number in the Articles links to its entry, and a check fails if they disagree.

**Where it strains:** Many numbers rest on precedents far smaller than the Commonwealth (strain 11 in [pressure points](../thoughts/pressure-points.md)). On the proposal's estimate, calling a vote of all terrans at 0.5% takes about 30 million signatures, against a Swiss share of about 0.9%. Seven numbers are not yet set, among them the protected budget shares and the voting age. Whether the defaults are right is open ([open questions](../OPEN-QUESTIONS.md)), and the [Charter's own first question](../CHARTER.md#open-questions-for-the-next-draft) asks it of every number.

**Read more:** [the government design](../proposals/modern-terran-government.md).

## Schedules 1 and 2 — Safeguards and Settings

A safeguard (Schedule 1) is a number a passing majority could use to switch off a check on itself, so it changes only as the Articles do (Part V.6). A setting (Schedule 2) tunes how the Commonwealth works, and a planetary act that is not urgent may move it within a range that binds; a safeguard's range is only advice.

**Why it says this:** The failure-modes research shows capture running through ordinary statutes. In Poland no party held two-thirds, so capture ran through statutes instead; from 2017 politicians chose most of the judicial council ([failure modes](../research/failure-modes.md)). Madrid's new majority abolished its body drawn by lot in 2020, after a party-line committee vote ([sortition at scale](../research/sortition-at-scale.md)). On 29 June 2026 the US Supreme Court made most independent regulators' heads removable at will, though not the Federal Reserve's [dla-piper/2026_slaughter-and-cook]. Since setting ranges bind and "this Charter" covers the Schedules, the Court's check before each vote (3.4) covers them too.

**Where it strains:** Entrenching a check also slows its repair: Hungary's repair in 2026 needed the same two-thirds majority as its capture in 2010. Where the line falls is a default to confirm. The voting age, for one, is a setting: a planetary act may move it only between 16 and 18, and a change waits until after the next voting day. The layers and this split answer the call for tiered entrenchment only in part, since a clerical repair still faces its layer's full bar ([open questions](../OPEN-QUESTIONS.md)). Hard rules also push change toward interpretation (strain 4 in [pressure points](../thoughts/pressure-points.md)).

**Read more:** [failure modes](../research/failure-modes.md), [executive and administration](../research/executive-and-administration.md).

## Schedule 3 — World Regions

Schedule 3 will list the world regions the Charter counts: at least ten, starting from an existing statistical classification, with each terran counted where they live. Regions count in six places, from the spread of petitions (3.1, 3.5) and the Council of Communities' seats (2.10) to the double majority that changes the Articles (Part V.5).

**Why it says this:** Spread rules stop one region deciding for all. The European Citizens' Initiative requires minimum signatures in seven states, and Nigeria's president needs a quarter of the votes in two-thirds of states ([deliberation](../research/deliberation.md), [scale and federalism](../research/scale-and-federalism.md)). Nations make a poor unit at this scale: with 1,000 seats shared by population, 116 of 217 countries and territories get under one expected seat. The government design asks for a fixed set of at least ten regions, and an existing classification would do at first ([the government design](../proposals/modern-terran-government.md)). Regions change only as the Foundations do, as in the second draft. Region lines decide whose majorities count when the Articles change, so a majority that could redraw them by the Articles' own bar could entrench itself.

**Where it strains:** Regions differ several-fold in size, so a double majority gives small regions weight and lets them block, the failure the research names for spread rules. None are chosen yet, so the Articles cannot change until they are. A quarter of ten regions is two and a half, and the Charter's own open questions ask how shares round. The [open questions](../OPEN-QUESTIONS.md) also ask how "votes" are defined; Part V.5 answers only the spread across regions. The [pressure points](../thoughts/pressure-points.md) predate the regions.

**Read more:** [scale and federalism](../research/scale-and-federalism.md), [deliberation](../research/deliberation.md).

## Schedule 4 — The Boundaries

Schedule 4 records, for each boundary in Part III, the Earth Science Panel's range and the limit a ceiling act sets within it. The Panel has not met, so neither is set.

**Why it says this:** Boundary values are precautionary judgements that move: precaution puts each "at the lower end of the zone of increasing risk" [richardson/2023_six-of-nine], and since 2023 the land boundary has risen from 75% to 78% of original forest. So the Panel publishes a range for each boundary, and ceiling acts choose within it. Today's measures, and where the planet stood in the 2026 Planetary Health Check [pik/2026_planetary-health-check]:

| Boundary | Measured by | 2026 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Climate change | CO₂ concentration, radiative forcing | Crossed: 426 ppm against 350 |
| Ocean acidification | Surface aragonite saturation | Crossed since 2025 |
| Introduction of novel entities | Synthetic chemicals released untested | Crossed |
| Biogeochemical flows | Nitrogen and phosphorus flows | Crossed, except ocean phosphorus |
| Freshwater change | Disturbed river flow, soil moisture | Crossed |
| Land-system change | Forest area, share of original | Crossed: 64% against 78% |
| Change in biosphere integrity | Extinction rate, human share of plant growth | Crossed |
| Atmospheric aerosol loading | Aerosol gap between hemispheres | Inside, though over in South Asia |
| Stratospheric ozone depletion | Stratospheric ozone | Inside |

**Where it strains:** With seven of nine crossed, limits mean little without fair shares and timelines, and the sharing principle is open (strain 3 in [pressure points](../thoughts/pressure-points.md)). Whether the Panel should report more often than every four years, when the checks are yearly, is open ([open questions](../OPEN-QUESTIONS.md)).

**Read more:** [floor and ceiling](../research/floor-and-ceiling.md), [polycentric governance](../research/polycentric-governance.md).

## Schedule 5 — Hazards

Schedule 5 lists three hazards for which the Stewards may declare an emergency (6.1): a fast-spreading disease; a weapon of mass destruction used, threatened, stolen or lost; and a crisis in the Commonwealth's own money. Peaceful protest or dissent, a vote, a draw or their result, and a slow breach of the ceiling are never hazards.

**Why it says this:** Emergencies are the shortest path to rule by decree, and across up to 122 countries, easier declarations meant more harm to basic rights [bjornskov-voigt/2021_emergencies-misuse]. Each hazard falls within the planetary powers (2.4). Disease is a weakest-link threat, where the least capable member sets the level of protection ([scale and federalism](../research/scale-and-federalism.md)). The euro needed emergency backstops ([money](../research/money.md)). Strasbourg found no real emergency behind Yerevan's post-election protests. Only a planetary act that is not urgent may change the list, or an urgent act could add a hazard and an emergency follow the same day.

**Where it strains:** The shared identity and voting systems are left off the list, though their failure would be serious. Article 4 meets it with audits, paper records and split keys, and an emergency there would put the Stewards over the offices that count the votes. What is never a hazard changes only as the Articles do, but the list itself can grow by planetary act. A crisis can still be manufactured or prolonged, the threat model's residual risk. The [pressure points](../thoughts/pressure-points.md) predate the emergency clause, and [the Charter's own open questions](../CHARTER.md#open-questions-for-the-next-draft) ask whether the list is right.

**Read more:** [failure modes](../research/failure-modes.md), [scale and federalism](../research/scale-and-federalism.md).
