# Commentary on the Foundations

*Not binding. Why the Foundations say what they say, with the evidence behind them · drafted by Claude from the research notes, 12 October 2026 · where this reading and Kara's intent differ, hers governs*

The research notes and thoughts cite the second working draft's numbering, in which Part IV here was Part VI and Part V was Part XI; the [changelog](../CHANGELOG.md#third-working-draft--12-october-2026) maps the rest.

## Preamble

The Preamble says who the Charter is for and what it aims at. It names the problem, billions without enough while living systems are pushed past safe limits, and the answer: a floor, a ceiling, and freedom in between.

**Why it says this:** "A safe and just space for humanity" is the title of Kate Raworth's 2012 Oxfam paper, the source Part III credits [raworth/2012_safe-and-just-space]. "Billions without enough" holds: the global doughnut put median shortfall in 2022 at 35% of people, about 3 billion. The second draft dropped "toward collapse", which outran sources that speak of a rising risk of irreversible change. Seven of nine planetary boundaries were crossed as of September 2026 ([floor and ceiling](../research/floor-and-ceiling.md)). The ethics draw on the humanist manifestos and the Amsterdam Declaration, which pair reason with compassion and admit fallibility, and on the Earth Charter, which calls Earth's protection "a sacred trust". Paul Kurtz's "planetary humanism" is the nearest named ancestor ([lineage](../research/lineage.md)).

**Where it strains:** The Charter belongs to every terran "who chooses to live by it", yet Part I makes everyone a terran from birth and Part II binds every terran (strain 1 in [pressure points](../thoughts/pressure-points.md)). The Amsterdam Declaration, by contrast, declines to impose its view on all humanity. "For most of our history we have divided ourselves by nation" may read as anachronistic, since nations are fairly recent; the research flagged this but did not study it. The Charter says nothing on population, which Humanist Manifesto II and the Earth Charter address ([open questions](../OPEN-QUESTIONS.md)).

**Read more:** [lineage](../research/lineage.md), [floor and ceiling](../research/floor-and-ceiling.md), [commonwealth](../research/commonwealth.md).

## Part I — Terrans

Part I makes every human being a terran from birth, with standing no one can grant or revoke. It defines the Commonwealth by shared commitments, so that belonging never depends on a state.

**Why it says this:** Standing that depends on a state can be lost. UNHCR counted 4.5 million stateless people at the end of 2025, and UK orders stripping citizenship on public-good grounds rose from 14 in 2016 to 104 in 2017. The UDHR bars only arbitrary loss of nationality, so I.1 goes further ([commonwealth](../research/commonwealth.md)). I.2 follows the mainstream scholarship: nations could not exist without mass literacy and print, which let strangers see themselves as one people [miller/2025_sep-nationalism]. The first draft had this backwards. Faster communication built more nations, and UN membership rose from 51 states in 1945 to 193 in 2011. So I.2 claims only that today's networks make a planetary bond possible.

**Where it strains:** Principle IV denies "the Charter's protection" to those who take others' freedom without just cause. That reads as partial forfeiture, against I.1's irrevocable standing and the fair hearing in Article 7.4 (strain 1 in [pressure points](../thoughts/pressure-points.md)). A voting identity still needs rules for fraud, duplicates and death that never amount to revocation. I.4 defines the Commonwealth without territory, yet nations join it territorially (Article 9), and land, water and air stay territorial. Whether the Commonwealth's authority follows people, territory or both is open ([open questions](../OPEN-QUESTIONS.md)).

**Read more:** [commonwealth](../research/commonwealth.md), [lineage](../research/lineage.md), [polycentric governance](../research/polycentric-governance.md).

## Part II — The Seven Principles

Part II sets seven principles that bind every terran and every institution: Compassion, Justice, Autonomy, Freedom, Knowledge, Repair, and Spirit over Letter. They are the values every other layer serves, and V.7 bars any amendment that weakens a terran's protection under them.

**Why it says this:** The Seven Principles adapt The Satanic Temple's Seven Fundamental Tenets, following them in number, order and substance [tst/2026_about-us]. Verbatim overlap is limited to short phrases, chiefly "compassion, wisdom, and justice" and "laws and institutions". The Charter turns the tenets' "should" into duties, adds the mind to bodily autonomy, extends Repair to institutions and to the Charter itself, and makes Spirit over Letter a rule for reading a constitution ([lineage](../research/lineage.md)). Fallibility and revisable values also run through the humanist manifestos and the Amsterdam Declaration. The drafting-session research recommended entrenching the Principles so that no majority could override them ([research to charter](../thoughts/research-to-charter.md)).

**Where it strains:** In a creed, forfeiting freedom is a moral warning. In a constitution, Principle IV's forfeiture can strip legal protection, and it drops the tenet's "willfully" (strain 1 in [pressure points](../thoughts/pressure-points.md)). One option has a court set any limits, after fair process, in proportion to the harm, with an intent element. Principle VII lets spirit override the text, which, with a hard amendment rule, moves change toward the Court (strain 4). Whether the Principles bind individuals or only institutions is open, and so is whether Principle V governs public decisions or private belief, given that I.3 lets faith flourish ([open questions](../OPEN-QUESTIONS.md)).

**Read more:** [lineage](../research/lineage.md), [living charter](../research/living-charter.md).

## Part III — The Floor and the Ceiling

Part III adapts Kate Raworth's doughnut into the purpose the economy serves and the test every institution answers (Article 2.7): every terran inside it, between a floor of eleven social needs and a ceiling of nine planetary boundaries.

**Why it says this:** The boundaries carry their official names, because "freshwater use" was retired in 2023 and the verdict flips under its replacement ([floor and ceiling](../research/floor-and-ceiling.md)). Boundary values are judgements: setting a safe distance "involves normative judgments of how societies choose to deal with risk and uncertainty" [rockstrom/2009_planetary-boundaries-ecology-society], and the values were revised in 2025 and 2026. So an Earth Science Panel publishes a range every four years and ceiling acts choose within it (Article 3.6), as the Montreal Protocol's science reviews feed binding adjustments ([polycentric governance](../research/polycentric-governance.md)). With seven of nine boundaries crossed as of September 2026, a plain ban on crossing would bind everyone or no one, so the Charter adds fair shares with timelines.

**Where it strains:** The floor can only rise and the ceiling follows science, yet a 2018 study found no country meeting basic needs within sustainable resource use (strain 3 in [pressure points](../thoughts/pressure-points.md)). Studies that fit everyone inside assume deep cuts at the top. The sharing principle is open, and so is whether the Panel should report more often, since Planetary Health Checks are yearly ([open questions](../OPEN-QUESTIONS.md)). Today's boundary setting is expert-led, with weak Global South support, and the "scientific bodies" that nominate Guardians are unnamed, so strain 2 is answered only in part.

**Read more:** [floor and ceiling](../research/floor-and-ceiling.md), [polycentric governance](../research/polycentric-governance.md).

## Part IV — A Utopia of Utopias

Part IV lets every terran pursue their own good life, alone or with others, and leave any community. The Commonwealth chooses no utopia for anyone, and each utopia ends where it would push another below the floor, take another's freedom, or push the planet past its ceiling.

**Why it says this:** A utopia of utopias draws on Robert Nozick's framework for utopia, and IV.1 echoes his "individually or jointly pursued". No planner could know what utopia is, so voluntary communities with free entry and exit work as a discovery procedure [mack/2026_nozick-political-philosophy]. Nozick called the framework "equivalent to" the minimal state and rejected patterned principles of distribution. The lineage note counts the floor, the wealth limit and planetary revenue among the patterns he wrote against. It offers one answer for the Charter: the ceiling protects the base every community needs, and the floor makes exit real, since, as Susan Okin argues, exit is weakest for girls and women raised inside a community ([lineage](../research/lineage.md)). The note relied on secondary accounts of Nozick.

**Where it strains:** Secular 19th-century US communes lasted 6.4 years on average, against 25.3 for religious ones, so exit protects less than it seems (strain 6 in [pressure points](../thoughts/pressure-points.md)). Libertarian readers will call the floor an imposed utopia, and requiring all Seven Principles of every community goes beyond Nozick. The right to move into any community (Article 2.9) sits against communities built on a shared vision; whether they may set entry conditions, and what they owe children and leavers, is open ([open questions](../OPEN-QUESTIONS.md)).

**Read more:** [lineage](../research/lineage.md), [deliberation](../research/deliberation.md).

## Part V — A Living Charter

Part V says the Charter can be wrong and lets each layer change at its own pace. The Foundations prevail over the Articles, the Articles over the Schedules, and all three over planetary acts, while this Commentary binds no one.

**Why it says this:** The old single rule, two-thirds in two votes a year apart, treated a clerical fix like a rights change and is harder than any precedent found. In Nevada's two-vote system the yes share fell on the second vote in 14 of 17 cases, by 8.7 points on average, though 13 of the 17 still passed [fisher/2024_nevada-two-election-requirement]. V.4 keeps that bar for the Foundations alone, a tiered entrenchment like South Africa's. V.5 asks majorities in two votes a year apart for the Articles, like Nevada's two votes, and in each a majority of regions, as Switzerland asks a majority of voters and of cantons ([living charter](../research/living-charter.md)). V.6 lets panels tune settings within fixed ranges, while region lines, which decide whose majorities count, stay at the Foundations' bar. V.7 follows the post-1989 trend of shielding rights from amendment, and binds every layer. The 25-year review (V.8) is a revision commission like Florida's, but drawn by lot.

**Where it strains:** The tiers cut both ways. A clerical repair to the Foundations still needs two-thirds twice, while every check now in the Articles, from emergency limits to the right to leave, needs only majorities in two votes, where it used to need two-thirds in both. Whether V.7 protects itself, whether to allow a wholesale rewrite, and whether 25 years between reviews is too long are open ([open questions](../OPEN-QUESTIONS.md)). Principle VII and open standing still move change toward the Court, as India's basic-structure doctrine shows (strain 4 in [pressure points](../thoughts/pressure-points.md)). After stage three a community may leave only if safeguards fail (Article 9.8), so Roznai's warning applies: blocked legal change can push toward revolution (strain 6).

**Read more:** [living charter](../research/living-charter.md).
